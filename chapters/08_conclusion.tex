\chapter{Conclusion}\label{chap:conclusion}

This chapter restates what the study set out to do, what it found, what the findings
answer, and what they do not. The evidence is in \cref{chap:results-evaluation}; nothing
is re-derived here.


\section{Summary of Findings}\label{sec:summary-findings}

Dataspace connectors are compared on features far more often than on performance, and the
comparisons that do exist rarely hold the identity layer constant
(\cref{sec:gap-analysis}). This thesis measured three \gls{dsp}-conformant connectors --
the Fraunhofer ISST \gls{edc}, the Factory-X \gls{edc}, and DSP-Native BaSyx -- on a
normalised single-host substrate, each deployed twice: once with its full \gls{dcp}
identity stack running, and once with identity verification mocked. The difference between
those two deployments is a direct measurement of what identity costs, rather than an
inference from systems that differ in several respects at once.

The campaign comprises 130 measured runs across eight scenarios and six connector--arm
combinations. Four results stand out.

\emph{The two \gls{edc} connectors are indistinguishable at the operating point, and
DSP-Native BaSyx is faster.} With identity enabled, a complete transaction takes
4672.5\,ms on the Fraunhofer ISST \gls{edc} and 4649.0\,ms on Factory-X -- a difference of
0.5\,\%, well inside their own run-to-run variation -- against 2884.5\,ms on BaSyx.

\emph{Identity costs a fixed amount per request, and only in the control plane.} Measured
on the provider itself, it adds 30.0\,ms, 41.3\,ms and 44.7\,ms respectively. That cost
does not grow with the work a request performs: across a thousandfold increase in catalog
size it stays within a narrow band while the underlying latency rises, so its relative
weight falls as requests get larger. The data plane shows no difference at all.

\emph{The protocol, not the identity layer, sets the baseline.} Between 93 and 97\,\% of
every transaction is spent waiting for the two asynchronous state machines to reach their
terminal states. The synchronous steps -- initiating a negotiation, initiating a transfer,
pulling the data -- together account for one to two per cent.

\emph{Capacity differs sharply, and the host was never the limit.} All six arms track
offered load to 2.5\,tx/s. Beyond that Factory-X delivers only half of what is offered,
the Fraunhofer ISST \gls{edc} plateaus near 4\,tx/s, and BaSyx continues to climb. Host
\gls{cpu} peaked between 32 and 48\,\% in every case, so each ceiling is a property of the
connector rather than of the machine.

Beyond the measurements, the study contributes the instrument that produced them: a
parity-audited benchmark harness, byte-identical across three connector repositories, with
a documented analysis pipeline in which every reported number is traceable to an immutable
result folder (\cref{sec:data-analysis,sec:reproducibility}).


\section{Answers to Research Questions}\label{sec:answers-rq}

\textbf{TRQ1 -- How do current production-track \gls{edc} implementations compare in
end-to-end performance under realistic dataspace data-exchange workloads?}

They are closer than their differing origins suggest at a realistic operating point, and
they diverge under load. At 1\,tx/s the two \gls{edc}-derived connectors are
indistinguishable from each other, and DSP-Native BaSyx completes the same transaction in
roughly 60\,\% of their time (\cref{sec:rq1}). Under rising load the ordering changes:
Factory-X is the first to stop converting offered load into completed transactions, at
about 2.5\,tx/s; the Fraunhofer ISST \gls{edc} sustains roughly 4\,tx/s before plateauing;
and BaSyx continues to deliver at every rung tested (\cref{sec:rq2}). Resource consumption
separates them further, with garbage-collection activity differing by nearly two orders of
magnitude between the \gls{edc} connectors and BaSyx, while none of the three approached
the host's capacity (\cref{sec:rq3}). No connector showed heap growth that would exhaust
its container over a thirty-minute run.

\textbf{TRQ2 -- Which architectural and implementation-level factors most strongly drive
the observed performance differences?}

Three factors account for most of what was measured, in decreasing order of effect.

The dominant one is the \emph{asynchronous protocol design itself}, which is common to all
three implementations and therefore explains the baseline rather than the differences
between them: contract negotiation and transfer are state machines whose terminal states a
client must wait for, and that waiting is 93--97\,\% of every transaction
(\cref{sec:rq4}). No implementation choice within a connector can move a figure that is
set by the protocol it implements.

The second is \emph{where each implementation performs credential verification}. On both
\gls{edc} connectors the identity cost concentrates almost entirely in the catalog request
-- the first protected message of an exchange -- while the subsequent asynchronous waits
are barely affected. On DSP-Native BaSyx the same cost appears inside those waits instead.
This is consistent with the \gls{edc} stack verifying a presentation once per exchange and
then proceeding against an established context, and with BaSyx validating per message
(\cref{sec:x1-overhead}); the per-hop traces collected during the campaign are the
evidence that would confirm it directly.

The third is \emph{deployment shape}. DSP-Native BaSyx collapses control plane and data
plane into a single process and serves the transfer synchronously, which is the most
plausible account of both its lower baseline latency and its very different
garbage-collection profile (\cref{sec:sut,sec:rq3}). That connector's comparison against
the other two is, however, the weakest of the three: its two arms do not share a consumer,
so only its provider-side figures are directly comparable (\cref{subsec:tv-internal}).


\section{Limitations}\label{sec:conclusion-limitations}

\Cref{sec:results-validity} states what the reported numbers measure and what could have
biased the comparison between the two arms of a connector. This section states the limits
of the study itself: how far its results carry beyond the setup that produced them, and
what may be concluded from them.

\subsection{How Far the Results Carry}\label{subsec:tv-external}

Everything was measured on one machine, with every component running beside every other
and communicating over a container network rather than a real one. Absolute latencies are
therefore lower than a distributed deployment would show. What transfers is the
\emph{ordering} of the connectors and the shape of their curves, not the millisecond
values.

The workload is also narrower than a production dataspace. One asset type and one dataset
shape were used; the protocol coverage is the negotiate--transfer--pull cycle; and policy
evaluation beyond the membership credential was never exercised. Tractus-X \gls{edc} could
not be brought to instrumentation parity in the time available, so the comparison covers
three of the four implementations introduced in \cref{subsec:different-connectors}.

The observation window bounds the stability result in the same way. A trend fitted over
thirty minutes is sufficient to show that no component grows fast enough to exhaust its
heap on that timescale, and insufficient to exclude a slow leak; the heap slopes reported
in \cref{sec:rq3} are to be read on those terms.

\subsection{What May Be Concluded}\label{subsec:tv-conclusion}

No significance test appears anywhere in \cref{chap:results-evaluation}. Five repetitions
of a run whose internal transactions are not independent of one another cannot support
one~\citep{hoefler2015scientific}, and a test computed anyway would be driven by its own
assumptions rather than by the data.

Differences are instead judged against the run-to-run range, and where the ranges of two
arms overlap the result is reported as inconclusive rather than as a small effect. The
\emph{separated} column of \cref{tab:x1-overhead} records only whether two observed ranges
are disjoint. That is weak evidence, and it is not a claim of statistical significance.
