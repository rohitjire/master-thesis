\chapter{Implementation and Test Setup}\label{chap:implementation}

This chapter realises the benchmarking design of \cref{chap:methodology} as a concrete, reproducible deployment. Three connector implementations are deployed on a single dedicated benchmark host, each in two Docker Compose deployments -- an identity-enabled arm for goal $G_1$ and an identity-disabled arm for goal $G_2$ -- that are identical in every layer except the identity stack. A shared observability pipeline instruments every runtime with the same agent, and a single k6 load driver, byte-identical across all connector repositories, generates the workloads. \Cref{sec:sut} names the systems under test and their versions, \cref{sec:test-env} describes the hardware and the software substrate, \cref{sec:deployment} details the two deployment arms and the parity between them, \cref{sec:data-tools} presents the observability stack and the reasons for its composition, \cref{sec:workload-gen} describes the load generator, the benchmark scenarios, and the run orchestration, and \cref{sec:reproducibility} states what a replication of the study requires.


\section{Systems Under Test}\label{sec:sut}

Of the four connector implementations introduced in \cref{subsec:different-connectors}, three are realised in the experimental setup: the Factory-X \gls{edc}, the Fraunhofer ISST \gls{edc}, and DSP-Native BaSyx. Tractus-X \gls{edc} could not be brought to instrumentation parity with the other three within the time frame of this work and is therefore excluded from the deployed setup; the comparison in the remainder of the thesis covers the three instrumented implementations.

The three systems divide into two deployment shapes. The Factory-X and Fraunhofer ISST deployments are symmetric two-participant dataspaces: a consumer and a provider, each consisting of an \gls{edc} control plane and data plane, exchange catalog, negotiation, and transfer messages over the \gls{dsp}~\citep{datapsaceProtocol2025}. DSP-Native BaSyx is asymmetric by construction: the provider is a single Spring Boot application that embeds the \gls{aas} repository and speaks \gls{dsp} natively, so control plane and data plane collapse into one process and the data transfer is a synchronous request against the \gls{aas} \gls{api}~\citep{dspNativeBasyx}. Its consumer side, in the identity-enabled arm, is a Factory-X \gls{edc} control plane. This structural asymmetry is not a defect of the setup but a property of the system under test, and it becomes explanatory input for the architectural analysis X2 defined in \cref{sec:gqm-decomposition}.

Two consequences of this environment shape the experiment design. First, all services of one deployment -- both participants, their databases, the monitoring stack, and the load driver -- are co-located on this single host, so the host itself can saturate before the connector does; the host-level exporter of \cref{subsec:host-metrics} exists to make that ceiling visible rather than let it masquerade as a connector limit. Second, because the connectors share the host and the monitoring backend, they are measured strictly one at a time, as required by the design commitment stated in \cref{sec:research-methodology}: at no point do two connector deployments run concurrently, and no other workloads are scheduled on the machine during measurement windows.

\subsection{Software Substrate}\label{subsec:substrate}

Every deployment arm is a single Docker Compose file, checked into the respective connector repository, that describes the complete stack: connector runtimes, identity infrastructure, databases, secret store, and monitoring~\citep{dockerComposeDocs}. Docker Compose on one host was chosen over a Kubernetes cluster deliberately. The study is single-host by design (\cref{subsec:hardware}), so an orchestrator would add a scheduling layer whose placement and restart decisions vary between runs without adding any capability the experiment needs; with Compose, service co-location is deterministic and the entire deployment topology is auditable by reading one YAML file per arm. This auditability carries the fairness argument of \cref{sec:comparing-benchmarks} directly into the artefacts: verifying that two arms differ only in the identity layer reduces to diffing two version-controlled Compose files.

Resource discipline is enforced uniformly at two levels. At the container level, every service carries an explicit Compose memory limit -- 768\,MB for control planes and data planes, 512\,MB for identity services and infrastructure. At the process level, every \gls{jvm} is started with fixed heap bounds via \texttt{JAVA\_TOOL\_OPTIONS}: \texttt{-Xms512m -Xmx768m} for control and data planes and \texttt{-Xms256m -Xmx512m} for identity services, identical across connectors and across arms. Both limits are part of the Compose files and therefore of the audited configuration.

\section{Deployment Architecture}\label{sec:deployment}

The central structural property of the setup is deployment parity: for each connector, the identity-enabled arm ($G_1$) and the identity-disabled arm ($G_2$) share the same host, the same resource limits, the same databases and secret store, the same monitoring stack, the same seeded dataset, and the same driver -- the only permitted difference is the identity layer. Parity is what licenses reading the per-connector difference $G_1 - G_2$ as the identity overhead X1 rather than as an artefact of two divergent deployments (\cref{sec:gqm-decomposition}); it operationalises Jain's factor-control requirement and the fairness attribute of the benchmarking standard~\citep{jain1991art, Ralph2020}. \Cref{fig:identity-onoff-parity} depicts the principle and the per-connector mechanism.

\subsection{Identity-Enabled Arm (G1): MVD-Based Deployments}\label{subsec:mvd}

The identity-enabled arm of each connector is a \gls{mvd}: the smallest deployment in which the full \gls{dcp} trust chain runs end to end~\citep{eclipseEdcIdentityHub}. Beyond the two participants, it comprises an issuer service, which issues the membership credential that the dataspace requires, and one Identity Hub per participant, which stores the participant's \glspl{vc} and answers presentation queries. Every \gls{dsp} exchange then carries the full cryptographic cost of \gls{dcp}: the requesting connector obtains a self-issued token from its \gls{sts}, the receiving connector resolves the counterparty's \gls{did} document, queries the presented \gls{vp}, and verifies credential signatures before the request is admitted~\citep{dcpSpec}.

The identity infrastructure ships empty, so the dataspace must be provisioned once per deployment before any transfer can succeed: the issuer is registered as trusted, both participants are created in their Identity Hubs, membership credentials are requested and stored, and the \gls{sts} client secrets are written to Vault. This provisioning is fully scripted (\cref{subsec:k6-harness}) and is part of the deployment procedure, not of the measured window.

Concretely, the Fraunhofer ISST arm runs the \gls{dcp} control-plane and federated-catalog assemblies together with two Identity Hubs, the issuer service, and an nginx instance serving the static \texttt{did:web} documents; the Factory-X arm runs its issuer service and two Identity Hubs next to the two control planes and two data planes; and the BaSyx arm runs the same Factory-X identity infrastructure, an \gls{edc} consumer control plane, and the BaSyx provider configured with its \gls{dcp}-backed validation service (\texttt{validationservice=mvd}), which verifies \gls{jwt} signatures, resolves \glspl{did}, and queries the membership \gls{vp} for every incoming \gls{dsp} request.

\subsection{Identity-Disabled Arm (G2): Mock Identity}\label{subsec:mock-arm}

In the identity-disabled arm, verification is short-circuited rather than removed: a mock identity service still issues and accepts a token, but no signature verification, \gls{did} resolution, or credential-service round trip takes place (\cref{sec:gqm-decomposition}). The issuer and the Identity Hubs are absent from these deployments entirely. Because the three connectors wire identity into their stacks differently, the mechanism that disables it necessarily differs per connector; the three mechanisms are described here and their heterogeneity is carried as an explicit construct-validity caveat into \cref{chap:results-evaluation}.

\textbf{Fraunhofer ISST \gls{edc}: runtime swap.} The upstream base control plane bundles the stock \texttt{org.eclipse.edc:iam-mock} extension, and the \gls{dcp} assembly explicitly excludes it in favour of the real identity stack. The identity-disabled arm therefore requires no code at all: the Compose file substitutes \texttt{controlplane-persistence} (and the matching federated-catalog assembly) for the \texttt{-dcp} variants. One pitfall was deliberately avoided: the repository's stock \texttt{iam-mock} Compose file runs in-memory stores and no monitoring, so using it as the $G_2$ arm would change persistence and observability along with identity and confound the comparison. The benchmark's $G_2$ Compose file instead replicates the $G_1$ topology -- PostgreSQL, Vault, and the full monitoring stack -- and swaps only the two runtime images.

\textbf{Factory-X \gls{edc}: benchmark-specific mock extension.} Factory-X compiles its \gls{dcp} integration into the control-plane distribution, and its transfer path expects an \gls{sts} to be present, so neither removing the identity modules nor substituting the stock \texttt{iam-mock} produces a bootable runtime. This work therefore adds a small extension, \texttt{fx-mock-identity}, that supplies the missing services in process: a \texttt{MockVcIdentityService} implementing the \gls{edc} \texttt{IdentityService} interface -- it embeds a membership credential into every outgoing token and verifies incoming tokens by parsing them back, with no signature check, no \gls{did} resolution, and no credential-service query -- together with a local \gls{sts}, so the transfer path finds the token service it expects. The extension is packaged as the sole identity provider into a dedicated control-plane assembly, \texttt{edc-controlplane-\allowbreak postgresql-\allowbreak hashicorp-\allowbreak vault-\allowbreak fxmock}, and the $G_2$ Compose file differs from $G_1$ only in using that image and omitting the identity infrastructure. The data plane runs the same image in both arms, its token-refresh settings pointing at an unused placeholder in the identity-disabled arm; the residual risk that identity work remains on the data path is checked empirically by the payload sweep (\cref{subsec:scenarios}) and discussed in \cref{chap:results-evaluation}.

\textbf{DSP-Native BaSyx: validation-service switch.} The embedded \texttt{dataspace-\allowbreak protocol-\allowbreak lib} selects its token validator by configuration~\citep{dspProtocolLib}: the $G_2$ arm sets the library property \texttt{org.factoryx.\allowbreak library.\allowbreak validationservice} to \texttt{mock}, selecting a \texttt{MockValidationService} that accepts a plain JSON token of the form \texttt{\{clientId, audience\}} without any cryptographic processing. The provider process is otherwise identical to the $G_1$ arm, including the patched image and the MongoDB, Keycloak, and MQTT internals. The mock token format is, however, wire-incompatible with the \gls{dcp} tokens that an \gls{edc} consumer emits, so the \gls{edc} consumer control plane cannot drive this arm. The $G_2$ deployment therefore replaces it with a raw-\gls{dsp} driver -- the k6 client speaks the \gls{dsp} \gls{api} of the provider directly, and a lightweight callback sink receives the asynchronous \gls{dsp} callbacks that a control plane would normally consume. The consumer side of the BaSyx pair thus differs between the arms as well as the provider side; consequences for interpreting the BaSyx X1 delta are treated in \cref{chap:results-evaluation}.

\section{Observability Stack}\label{sec:data-tools}

\Cref{sec:metrics} requires two independent vantage points: the client view of the load generator and the server view of the systems under test. The server view is realised by one pipeline -- \gls{otel} instrumentation feeding Prometheus for metrics and Tempo for traces, with Grafana as the reading instrument -- deployed with byte-identical configuration in all three connector repositories.

\textbf{Why this composition.} The pipeline has three layers because \gls{otel} deliberately covers only the first of them. Instrumentation has to be identical across heterogeneous runtimes, because a measurement instrument that varies per connector reintroduces precisely the confound the fairness requirement forbids~\citep{Ralph2020}; \gls{otel} meets that condition as a vendor- and tool-agnostic \gls{cncf} standard, and it is explicitly not an observability backend, leaving storage and visualisation to other tools~\citep{otelWhatIs}. That boundary forces an explicit backend decision, which Prometheus answers on the three properties this study depends on: a dimensional data model queried through PromQL, which yields the per-service breakdown the analysis needs~\citep{prometheusOverview}; counter and histogram types that match the rate-error-duration view~\citep{prometheusMetricTypes}; and a range-query endpoint that evaluates an expression between an explicit start and end timestamp, which is what makes the time-aligned per-run snapshot of \cref{subsec:orchestration} possible~\citep{prometheusHttpApi}. Grafana supplies the frontend that \gls{otel} leaves open, over the metric and the trace store alike~\citep{grafanaDocs, grafanaTempoDocs}. Two alternatives were rejected on the same criteria: a hosted commercial observability service would move telemetry off the benchmark host and forfeit reproduction from pinned open images~\citep{papadopoulos2021reproducible}, and consuming each connector's own metrics endpoint would make the instrument a per-connector variable. The cost of instrumenting at all is acknowledged rather than assumed away -- monitoring frameworks impose measurable overhead on the application they observe~\citep{reichelt2021overhead} -- which is why the agent is itself subjected to the on-versus-off comparison of \cref{subsec:tracing}.

\subsection{Metrics Pipeline: OpenTelemetry, Prometheus, and Grafana}\label{subsec:metrics-pipeline}

\textbf{Instrumentation.} Every connector \gls{jvm} is instrumented by the \gls{otel} Java agent (version 2.8.0), attached at start-up via the \texttt{-javaagent} flag; the agent injects bytecode at class-loading time and requires no modification of the application~\citep{otelJavaAgent}. This zero-code property is the decisive fairness argument: the connectors under test are heterogeneous -- Jetty/Jersey-based \gls{edc} runtimes and a Spring Boot application -- yet the same agent instruments all of them, and the \gls{otel} semantic conventions guarantee that the resulting series carry the same names and semantics everywhere, such as \texttt{http\_server\_request\_duration} for server-side request latency and the \texttt{jvm\_*} series for heap, \gls{gc}, and thread telemetry~\citep{opentelemetrySemconv}. Each container sets a distinct \texttt{OTEL\_SERVICE\_NAME}, so every runtime is separable in every downstream query, and the agent exports over OTLP/gRPC every 5\,s.

\textbf{Collection and storage.} All agents push to a single \gls{otel} Collector, which batches the streams and re-exposes the metrics in Prometheus format, converting the \gls{otel} resource attributes into Prometheus labels so that per-runtime attribution survives the format boundary. Prometheus scrapes the collector and the node exporter every 5\,s, matching the agent export interval so that every scrape observes fresh samples. Retention covers one working week, which comfortably outlives the longest scenario and leaves the archived per-run snapshot, not the live database, as the artefact of record.

\textbf{Reading instrument.} Grafana provisions two dashboards from the repository: a rate-error-duration dashboard over the connector \gls{api} series and a host dashboard over the node-exporter series~\citep{grafanaDocs}. Grafana's role is deliberately limited to live inspection -- verifying during a run that the stack is healthy and that load arrives where expected. The measurement of record is never a dashboard screenshot: it is the k6 summary and the Prometheus snapshot CSVs archived per run (\cref{subsec:orchestration}), which reuse the same PromQL expressions as the dashboards, so what is archived equals what was observed live.

\subsection{Distributed Tracing}\label{subsec:tracing}

The same agent that produces metrics also emits spans for every instrumented operation, which the collector forwards to Grafana Tempo, a tracing backend running here as a single binary with local storage and a retention of one week~\citep{grafanaTempoDocs}. Traces serve one purpose in this study: decomposing the wall time of a \gls{dsp} transaction into its per-hop constituents -- consumer control plane, provider control plane, data plane -- which is the primary evidence feeding the architectural explanation X2 (\cref{sec:gqm-decomposition}). Trace data is queried through Grafana and consulted in the analysis, not gated upon during runs. Instrumentation is never free; the monitoring overhead is itself examined by comparing agent-on and agent-off runs, and the result is reported with the threats to validity in \cref{chap:results-evaluation}.

\subsection{Host-Level Metrics}\label{subsec:host-metrics}

The Prometheus node exporter publishes hardware and operating-system metrics of the benchmark host, of which the analysis uses CPU-busy time and available memory~\citep{prometheusNodeExporter}. Its role follows from the single-host design: when a latency knee appears in a saturation sweep, the host series decide whether the ceiling belongs to the connector or to the machine (\cref{subsec:hardware}). The stack deliberately omits a per-container metrics agent such as cAdvisor: the per-process resource view required by $G_k.\text{RQ}3$ -- heap occupancy, \gls{gc} pause rate, thread count -- is already delivered per runtime by the \gls{otel} agent's \gls{jvm} telemetry, and the host exporter bounds the total. Per run, eight server-side series are archived as CSV files: per-service heap, \gls{gc} rate, and thread count; per-service request throughput, 95th-percentile server latency, and error rate; and host CPU-busy and memory-used.


\section{Workload Generation}\label{sec:workload-gen}

\subsection{k6 Harness Architecture}\label{subsec:k6-harness}

Load is generated with Grafana k6, a scriptable load generator whose scenarios are JavaScript programs executed by configurable executors~\citep{k6docs}. Three properties motivated the choice. First, k6 provides open-model arrival-rate executors that fire requests on a clock, independently of how quickly the system responds; a closed loop, in which each virtual user waits for its previous transaction, lets a slow system throttle its own offered load and thereby hide the latency tail -- the classic open-versus-closed distortion~\citep{schroeder2006open}. Second, a \gls{dsp} transaction is not a single request but a multi-step, partially asynchronous protocol flow; k6 scripts express the full sequence -- catalog request, negotiation initiation, polling to the agreed state, transfer initiation, polling to the \gls{edr}, data pull -- as one measured transaction. Third, custom metrics, tags, and thresholds allow the harness to emit exactly the metric set defined in \cref{sec:metrics} and to enforce per-scenario validity gates.

The harness is maintained as a small software project rather than as a set of ad-hoc scripts, so that running an experiment is one reproducible command instead of a remembered procedure. \Cref{lst:harness-layout} shows its layout: a library layer carrying the protocol flow, the polling logic, the seeding helpers and the metric definitions; the seven scenario scripts; the orchestration and tooling scripts; and one configuration file per connector.

{\pagebreak}

\begin{lstlisting}[numbers=none, basicstyle=\scriptsize\ttfamily, columns=fixed, caption={Layout of the k6 harness, identical in all three connector repositories.}, label=lst:harness-layout]
k6-scripts/
  lib/               protocol flow, HTTP helpers, polling, seeding, metrics
  scenarios/         the seven load shapes
  config/            <connector>.json - the only per-connector file
  orchestration/     run.sh, run-matrix.sh, snapshot-prom.sh
  tools/             verify-parity.sh, gen-payloads.sh
  identity-setup.js  one-shot dataspace provisioning
  Makefile
\end{lstlisting}

The per-connector configuration is the only file that differs between repositories; it holds the management and \gls{dsp} endpoints, the participant identifiers, the seed asset, and the Prometheus address. Everything above it is a byte-identical copy, and a parity checker (\texttt{tools/verify-parity.sh}) diffs \texttt{lib/} and \texttt{scenarios/} against the canonical copy and refuses a run on any drift -- without that guard the duplicated load driver could itself become a confound. One driver therefore exercises six deployments -- three connectors, each in two arms -- selected only by the target configuration and the identity-mode setting. The single exception is the BaSyx $G_2$ arm, whose raw-\gls{dsp} driver variant replaces the \gls{edc} management flow for the reasons given in \cref{subsec:mock-arm}, reproducing the same load shapes and metric definitions against the \gls{dsp} endpoints directly.

\subsection{Deployment and Execution Workflow}\label{subsec:workflow}

Each connector repository exposes the same two entry points next to its Compose files, so that the operating procedure is identical whichever connector is under test. \texttt{install.sh} takes the identity arm as its only argument, selects the matching Compose file, and brings the stack up with monitoring; \texttt{cleanup.sh} tears it down together with its volumes, so that the next run starts from an empty database rather than from inherited state, and with no argument it removes both arms. Because the three stacks deliberately share container names and run one at a time on the same host (\cref{subsec:hardware}), \texttt{install.sh} additionally performs a pre-flight check: it removes containers left behind by a previously benchmarked connector and refuses to start while another connector's stack is still running, which turns a silent cross-connector collision into an explicit error.

The identity-enabled arms of Factory-X and BaSyx boot with empty Identity Hubs and must be provisioned once before any transaction can succeed -- registering the trusted issuer, creating both participants, requesting their membership credentials, and writing the \gls{sts} client secrets to Vault. That provisioning existed only as an interactive \gls{api} collection, which is unusable on a headless server, so it was ported to a one-shot k6 script requiring nothing beyond the k6 binary the scenarios already need. The Fraunhofer ISST arm instead ships pre-signed credentials alongside its static \gls{did} documents and needs no such step. \Cref{lst:run-workflow} shows the resulting lifecycle.

\begin{lstlisting}[numbers=none, basicstyle=\scriptsize\ttfamily, columns=fixed, caption={Lifecycle of one benchmark arm, from deployment to teardown.}, label=lst:run-workflow]
# 1  bring up one arm of one connector (stack + monitoring)
./install.sh on                  # identity-enabled arm  (G1)
./install.sh off                 # identity-disabled arm (G2)

# 2  provision the dataspace once - identity-enabled arm only
cd k6-scripts && k6 run identity-setup.js

# 3  run one scenario: connector, arm and scenario are the only variables
IDENTITY_MODE=on ./orchestration/run.sh factoryx steady
RATE=10 DURATION=5m IDENTITY_MODE=on ./orchestration/run.sh factoryx steady

# 4  or the whole matrix for this arm: every scenario, three repetitions
IDENTITY_MODE=on ./orchestration/run-matrix.sh factoryx 3

# 5  tear the arm down, volumes included
./cleanup.sh
\end{lstlisting}

Three variables therefore determine any measurement -- the connector, the identity arm, and the scenario -- and each appears exactly once on the command line. The orchestration script wraps the k6 invocation in the measurement pipeline and the archiving described in \cref{subsec:orchestration}. A scenario's load parameters -- offered rate, duration, payload or catalog size, poll interval -- are environment overrides on the same command, so any deviation from the defaults of \cref{tab:k6-scenarios} is explicit at the call site and is recorded in the run's provenance file. A Makefile exposes the same targets under shorter names for interactive use, and a payload generator produces the sized files that the data-plane sweep serves.

Identity is a factor, not a scenario: every scenario runs once against each arm of a connector with identical code and parameters, and each sample carries an \texttt{identity\_mode} tag, so the scenario set yields the paired measurements from which X1 is computed.

\texttt{smoke} \textbf{-- correctness gate and uncontended baseline.} A single user runs the full flow five times, and any failure fails the run outright, since without contention a failure indicates a broken stack rather than overload. Run against the $G_2$ arm it doubles as a parity probe: the transaction must succeed \emph{without} a valid credential, confirming that identity is genuinely bypassed.

\texttt{steady} \textbf{-- the realistic operating point.} A fixed offered rate is held well below saturation, which makes this the head-to-head comparison point for latency percentiles and sustained throughput. Its open-model executor keeps the tail honest under the coordinated-omission argument of \cref{subsec:k6-harness}~\citep{schroeder2006open}.

\texttt{saturation-open} \textbf{-- the scalability knee.} The offered rate is swept upward in stages to trace the full latency-versus-load curve. Its thresholds are abort-only guards, so the saturation knee and the onset of failure are read from the curve rather than from a pass/fail gate, consistent with the capacity relationship expressed by Little's law~\citep{little1961proof}.

\texttt{concurrency-closed} \textbf{-- the concurrency companion.} Concurrent virtual users are ramped instead of the arrival rate. This maps directly onto the intuitive question of how a connector behaves as more clients transfer at once, but as a closed model it is subject to coordinated omission and is therefore reported only alongside the open-model result, never as the sole scalability claim.

\texttt{payload-sweep} \textbf{-- data-plane throughput in isolation.} One contract is negotiated during setup, after which only the payload size varies. This isolates raw data-plane throughput from the control-plane handshake, following the one-factor-at-a-time principle of workload construction~\citep{kistowski2015benchmark}. Because identity work concentrates in the control plane, the expected identity overhead here is near zero, which makes this scenario the empirical check on the Factory-X data-plane caveat of \cref{subsec:mock-arm}.

\texttt{soak} \textbf{-- endurance and resource drift.} A gentle rate is applied for one hour to expose heap drift, \gls{gc} degradation, and thread growth that short runs cannot reveal, observed through the \gls{jvm} series~\citep{gregg2012thinking}.

\texttt{catalog-sweep} \textbf{-- control-plane scaling.} The provider is seeded with a controlled number of assets and each iteration issues a catalog-only request, with one run per catalog size. Seeding requires the management \gls{api} backed by SQL persistence, so this scenario targets the two \gls{edc} providers; BaSyx catalog scaling is treated separately.

Scenario thresholds play two distinct roles. Gate thresholds (on \texttt{smoke}, \texttt{steady}, \texttt{soak}, and the sweeps) invalidate a run that violates them -- for example, a transaction-failure rate above 1\,\% in \texttt{steady} -- ensuring latency statistics are only reported over essentially successful runs. Abort-only thresholds (on the two ramping scenarios) merely stop a run that has degenerated far past its knee; they are diagnostic bounds, not claims.

The two \texttt{time\_to\_*} trends realise the asynchronous-cycle measurement of \cref{subsec:primary-metrics}. Negotiation and transfer are asynchronous state machines: the client posts a request, receives a non-terminal state, and polls until the terminal state is reached~\citep{eclipseEdcDocumentation}. The harness polls at a fixed interval -- 250\,ms by default, recorded with every run -- without backoff, so the quantisation of the measured wait is uniform: each \texttt{time\_to\_*} value is an upper bound that overstates the true time by at most one poll interval. Every poll is also a real request against the control plane, so the harness counts polls per phase rather than hiding this observer effect; the counts accompany the latency figures in \cref{chap:results-evaluation}. For BaSyx, whose transfer is synchronous by architecture (\cref{sec:sut}), the \gls{edr} wait terminates on the first poll; \texttt{time\_to\_edr} is therefore reported as a real distribution only for the \gls{edc}-based connectors, and the asymmetry itself becomes input to X2.

\subsection{Run Orchestration and Measurement Window}\label{subsec:orchestration}

A single orchestration script executes one experiment -- one connector, one scenario, one arm -- as a fixed pipeline: parity check, health gate, warm-up, measured run, post-roll, server-side snapshot, provenance record. The health gate polls the consumer management \gls{api} and the Prometheus readiness endpoint before anything is measured, so a partially booted stack cannot silently produce degenerate numbers. The warm-up drives 60\,s of light load and is discarded, letting \gls{jvm} just-in-time compilation and cold caches settle outside the measured window -- one of the named common mistakes the design avoids~\citep{jain1991art}. After the measured run, a 15\,s post-roll guarantees that the final 5\,s scrape falls inside the observation window; the script then executes the range queries of \cref{subsec:host-metrics} over exactly the run's start and end epochs, archiving the server view time-aligned with the client view.

Every run terminates in an immutable result folder whose provenance file records all parameters needed to reproduce or audit it -- connector, scenario, arm, poll interval, catalog size, start and end epochs, the k6 version and exit code, the load knobs, and the git commit of the harness:

\begin{lstlisting}[numbers=none, basicstyle=\scriptsize\ttfamily, columns=fixed, caption={Per-run result folder produced by the orchestration script.}, label=lst:result-folder]
results/<connector>/<scenario>/<UTC-timestamp>/
  run.log          k6 console output of the measured run
  k6-summary.json  client-side metrics (p50/p95/p99 per trend)
  *.csv            eight server-side series from Prometheus
  meta.json        parameters, epochs, identity mode, git commit
\end{lstlisting}

A matrix script layers the repetition discipline on top: for one connector and one arm it executes each scenario at least three times, shuffling the scenario order per repetition to break ordering and thermal bias, and iterating the catalog sweep over its sizes~\citep{jain1991art, hoefler2015scientific}. Connector deployments are never run in parallel (\cref{subsec:hardware}). The complete run matrix is therefore three connectors $\times$ two arms $\times$ seven scenarios $\times$ at least three repetitions, executed one connector-arm at a time; repetitions are aggregated to medians with interquartile ranges before any figure in \cref{chap:results-evaluation} is drawn.


\section{Reproducibility}\label{sec:reproducibility}

The benchmarking standard requires documentation sufficient for replication~\citep{Ralph2020, empiricalStandardsBenchmarking}; this setup meets the requirement by making the repositories themselves the documentation. Everything an experiment consumes is version-controlled on one branch (\texttt{feat/final-setup}) of each connector repository: the Compose files of both arms, the monitoring configuration and dashboards, the mock-identity extension and assemblies, the k6 harness with all scenario parameters, and the orchestration scripts. Third-party images are pinned by tag (\cref{subsec:substrate}); first-party images are built from the repository, and the commit hash recorded in every run's provenance file ties each result to the exact source state that produced it. Each reported figure is therefore traceable to one or more immutable result folders, and every X1 value to the two paired folders it was differenced from.

Replicating a connector's measurement reduces to five steps on a comparable Docker host: clone the connector repository at the audited branch; build the first-party images; start the desired arm with its Compose file; for an identity-enabled arm, run the one-shot identity provisioning; and invoke the matrix script. The environment that produced the reported numbers is specified in \cref{sec:test-env}, and the principles this procedure follows -- controlled environment, repeated runs, distribution-based reporting -- are the established ones for reproducible performance evaluation~\citep{papadopoulos2021reproducible, hoefler2015scientific}. Residual nondeterminism remains: a shared physical host, \gls{jvm} just-in-time variability, and network jitter inside the container network are not eliminated but are mitigated by the discarded warm-up, the randomised repetitions, and median-based reporting, and they are quantified rather than assumed away by the host-level series of \cref{subsec:host-metrics}. The known deviations from full uniformity -- the untagged base images in one repository, the host-versus-bridge networking difference, and the BaSyx driver asymmetry -- are stated in \cref{sec:deployment,subsec:substrate} and revisited as threats to validity in \cref{chap:results-evaluation}.
