\chapter{Methodology}\label{chap:methodology}

This chapter turns the thesis-level research questions of \cref{sec:research-questions} into a measurable benchmarking design. \Cref{sec:comparing-benchmarks} selects and combines the methodological frameworks that govern this thesis, \cref{sec:gqm-decomposition} decomposes the research questions into goals, questions, and metrics, and \cref{sec:metrics} defines the performance metrics that the later chapters collect and interpret. \Cref{sec:research-summary} summarises.


\section{Research Methodology}\label{sec:research-methodology}

The objective stated in \cref{sec:research-objective} -- a systematic and reproducible performance comparison of four \gls{dsp} connector implementations -- is pursued here through a controlled benchmarking experiment. \textbf{TRQ1} and \textbf{TRQ2} are stated above the level of operational metrics by design. This chapter refines them into measurable questions and indicators using the \gls{gqm} framework~\citep{caldiera1994goal}. \Gls{gqm} derives every metric from an explicit measurement goal, so that no goal is left unmeasured and no metric is collected without a goal to justify it~\citep{minhas2023gqm}.

No single methodology covers the full path from a research goal to a credible result. This thesis therefore combines four established methods, each filling a gap the others leave:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt, topsep=4pt]
    \item the \gls{gqm} framework, for goal-to-metric traceability~\citep{caldiera1994goal};
    \item the experimental-design and statistical-analysis procedure of \emph{The Art of Computer Systems Performance Analysis}~\citep{jain1991art};
    \item the ACM SIGSOFT Empirical Standard for benchmarking, as a validity audit~\citep{Ralph2020, empiricalStandardsBenchmarking};
    \item ISO/IEC~25010, for the performance-efficiency vocabulary that disciplines metric selection~\citep{iso25010}.
\end{itemize}


Two design commitments shape everything downstream, and each answers a specific threat to the comparison.

The first is that every connector is benchmarked twice: once with its real identity and trust stack enabled, and once with identity verification mocked. The reason is that the four implementations differ most in exactly this layer, ranging from a full \gls{dcp} stack to a non-EDC validation service (\cref{subsec:different-connectors}). A single identity-enabled measurement per connector would therefore fold the cost of the identity stack together with every other architectural difference between the connectors, leaving the identity cost obtainable only by inference across implementations that differ in many respects at once. Measuring the same connector twice, with the identity layer as the only changed factor, replaces that inference with a within-connector difference in which host, workload, instrumentation, and connector build are held constant across the pair.

The second is that the connectors are measured one at a time on a normalised substrate. A shared host and a shared monitoring backend would let the load of one connector contaminate the measurements of another, and any observed connector-to-connector gap would then be unattributable. Sequential measurement costs wall-clock time, but it keeps the host a controlled factor instead of a variable shared between systems under test.


\section{Selecting and Combining Methodologies}\label{sec:comparing-benchmarks}

A benchmark is best understood, in the definition of \emph{Systems Benchmarking: For Scientists and Engineers}, as a standard measurement tool coupled with an evaluation methodology, not merely a load generator~\citep{kounev2020systems}. Choosing that methodology is itself a design decision, and several mature candidates exist. This section surveys them, groups them by the part of the benchmarking problem they address, and argues that none is individually sufficient for a fair, asynchronous, cross-implementation connector comparison -- which motivates the combined approach this thesis adopts.

\textbf{Goal-oriented measurement.} The \gls{gqm} framework, introduced in \emph{The Goal Question Metric Approach}, structures measurement top-down: a measurement goal is refined into questions, each of which is answered by metrics, so that every metric is traceable to a goal~\citep{caldiera1994goal}. It remains in active use for structuring empirical comparison~\citep{minhas2023gqm}. Its strength -- traceability -- is also its boundary: \gls{gqm} prescribes neither how to run a fair experiment nor how to analyse the results.

\textbf{Performance analysis and experiment design.} \emph{The Art of Computer Systems Performance Analysis} supplies that missing rigour through a defined sequence: state the goals and system boundaries, select metrics, parameters and factors, then design the experiments and analyse them. It adds a catalogue of common mistakes, among them omitting warm-up, reporting means instead of percentiles, and using unrepresentative workloads~\citep{jain1991art}. \emph{Experimentation in Software Engineering} frames such work as a controlled experiment with explicit scoping, planning, execution, analysis, and presentation phases~\citep{wohlin2012experimentation}. Neither, however, derives \emph{which} metrics matter to a given research goal, which is the question \gls{gqm} answers.

\textbf{Benchmarking-specific methodology.} A dedicated benchmarking literature addresses the construction of the benchmark itself~\citep{kounev2020systems, bermbach2017cloud}. \emph{Systems Benchmarking: For Scientists and Engineers} decomposes any benchmark into three elements -- metrics, workloads, and a measurement methodology -- and codifies quality criteria for each~\citep{kounev2020systems}. \emph{Cloud Service Benchmarking} adds a lifecycle view from the client's perspective, foregrounding repeatability, fairness, and understandability for services accessed over a network~\citep{bermbach2017cloud}, which is the situation of a connector measured through its \gls{api}. \emph{How to Build a Benchmark} stresses relevance in workload construction: a workload is meaningful only if it mirrors behaviour of interest~\citep{kistowski2015benchmark}. The quality of a finished benchmark can in turn be assessed against the five criteria set out in \emph{The Art of Building a Good Benchmark} -- relevance, repeatability, fairness, verifiability, and economy~\citep{huppler2009art}.

\textbf{Validity standards.} Whether a finished study counts as a credible benchmark is the domain of empirical standards~\citep{Ralph2020, hasselbring2021benchmarking}. The ACM SIGSOFT Empirical Standard for benchmarking enumerates five essential attributes: benchmark justification, workload specification, fairness to all systems under test, replication-sufficient documentation, and stability through repetition~\citep{Ralph2020, empiricalStandardsBenchmarking}. \emph{Benchmarking as Empirical Standard in Software Engineering Research} makes the independent case for treating benchmarking as a first-class empirical instrument~\citep{hasselbring2021benchmarking}. These are checklists: they audit a benchmark, but they do not build one.

\textbf{Statistical rigour and reproducibility.} Sound reporting is a recurring failure point. \emph{Scientific Benchmarking of Parallel Computing Systems} finds that performance results are frequently reported with too little statistical care to distinguish a real improvement from noise, and prescribes design and reporting rules to correct this~\citep{hoefler2015scientific}. \emph{Methodological Principles for Reproducible Performance Evaluation in Cloud Computing}~\citep{papadopoulos2021reproducible} and \emph{A Methodology for Scientific Benchmarking with Large-Scale Applications}~\citep{armstrong2001large} reinforce the same point: repeat the runs, report distributions rather than point values, and control the environment.

\textbf{Quality vocabulary.} Metric selection finally needs a shared vocabulary. ISO/IEC~25010 defines performance efficiency through three sub-characteristics -- time behaviour, resource utilisation, and capacity -- which map directly onto latency, resource consumption, and throughput~\citep{iso25010}.

Two further candidates are considered but not adopted as the organising spine. A purely hypothesis-driven design fixes the analysis before the exploratory measurements this thesis still requires, and a full factorial Design of Experiments multiplies the run matrix beyond what a one-connector-at-a-time schedule on a shared host can absorb~\citep{jain1991art}. Both inform the experiment design in \cref{chap:implementation} without dictating the structure of this chapter.

\textbf{The combined method.} The table makes the complementarity explicit. The \gls{gqm} framework ties every metric to a research goal, but says nothing about execution or credibility~\citep{caldiera1994goal}. The procedure of \emph{The Art of Computer Systems Performance Analysis}, reinforced by the reporting discipline of \emph{Scientific Benchmarking of Parallel Computing Systems}, supplies the constructive half: factor control, a discarded warm-up, repetition, and percentile rather than mean analysis~\citep{jain1991art, hoefler2015scientific}. The ACM SIGSOFT Empirical Standard and the benchmark quality criteria supply the evaluative half, auditing whether the constructed benchmark is fair, reproducible, and verifiable~\citep{Ralph2020, empiricalStandardsBenchmarking, huppler2009art}. The benchmarking-specific methodologies shape the metric, workload, and measurement choices in between~\citep{kounev2020systems, bermbach2017cloud}, and ISO/IEC~25010 fixes the metric vocabulary~\citep{iso25010}. Neither half is sufficient alone: a benchmark built without an audit may be rigorous and still not be credible, and an audit with nothing built is a checklist with nothing to check. Together they form a build-then-audit pipeline. \Gls{gqm} states what to measure, the performance-analysis procedure builds the experiment, and the empirical standard verifies that the construction clears the bar a credible benchmark must meet.

This discipline is the precondition for the central comparison of this thesis. The difference between an identity-enabled and an identity-disabled run, defined in \cref{sec:gqm-decomposition}, can be attributed to the identity layer only if every other factor is held constant across the pair. That is exactly the factor control prescribed for performance analysis, and the fairness requirement of the empirical standard~\citep{jain1991art, Ralph2020}. The combined method therefore licenses reading the measured difference as the cost of identity, not as noise.


\section{Goal--Question--Metric Decomposition}\label{sec:gqm-decomposition}

The benchmark carries two measurement goals that differ along a single axis -- the identity and trust layer -- and asks the same questions under each. Following the \gls{gqm} goal template of object, purpose, focus, viewpoint, and environment~\citep{caldiera1994goal}, the first goal, \textbf{G1} (performance with identity enabled), is:

\begin{description}
    \item[Object] the \gls{dsp} flow of catalog request, contract negotiation, transfer, and data pull;
    \item[Purpose] to characterise and compare;
    \item[Focus] time behaviour, capacity, and resource utilisation -- the performance-efficiency sub-characteristics of ISO/IEC~25010~\citep{iso25010};
    \item[Viewpoint] a connector operator or integrator;
    \item[Environment] four interoperable \gls{dsp} connector implementations -- Tractus-X EDC, Factory-X EDC, the Fraunhofer ISST \gls{edc}, and DSP-Native BaSyx -- each running its full \gls{dcp} identity stack on a normalised substrate.
\end{description}

The second goal, \textbf{G2} (performance with identity disabled), is identical in every clause except the environment: each connector runs with identity verification mocked. Identity disabled means verification is short-circuited, not removed. A mock identity service still issues and accepts a trivial token, but the cryptographic work of the \gls{dcp} no longer runs: secure-token exchange, DID-document resolution, and verifiable-credential presentation and verification~\citep{eclipseEdcIdentityHub}. The four implementations disagree most on exactly this layer, ranging from a full \gls{dcp} stack to a non-EDC validation service. Isolating it cleanly is what the rest of the thesis relies on. The set also spans more than the EDC core: DSP-Native BaSyx is an \gls{aas} application with native \gls{dsp} support, not an EDC build. The neutral term \emph{\gls{dsp} connector implementations} is therefore used when all four are meant~\citep{dam2024surveydataspaceconnectorimplementations}.

\Cref{fig:gqm-overview} summarises the resulting design, and reads from top to bottom. The top box is the population. Every connector in the benchmark passes through the whole diagram, so the figure states the design once instead of repeating it per connector. The middle row is the only place the two arms differ. $G_1$ runs the real \gls{dcp} identity stack; $G_2$ runs a mock validator, drawn dashed to mark it as the counterfactual arm. Both arms then converge on a single band of research questions, scenarios, metrics, and offered load. That shared band is what makes the two arms comparable, since anything varying between them other than identity would end up inside the difference. The bottom box is the one quantity that no single run produces, the per-connector, per-metric difference $G_1 - G_2$ reported as the identity overhead X1.

\textbf{The cross-goal analysis.} One analysis is produced by contrasting the goals rather than asked within one, which is why it is not numbered as a research question. \textbf{X1}, the identity overhead, is the per-connector, per-metric difference $G_1 - G_2$: for each question above, the identity-disabled value subtracted from the identity-enabled value. This is the headline result -- the directly measured cost of running real identity, in place of the inference to which an indirect approach would be limited. Interpreting X1 and the connector-to-connector gaps measured under $G_1$ against the documented design differences of each implementation is what answers \textbf{TRQ2}; that interpretation is reported with the results rather than treated as a separate measured output~\citep{evaluatingtheperformanceimpactofdatasovereignty, eclipseEdcIdentityHub}.

Tail percentiles are reported alongside medians because the mean conceals the tail that dominates user-perceived behaviour at scale~\citep{dean2013tail}.

\section{Performance Metrics}\label{sec:metrics}

The metrics fall into two tiers. Primary metrics are observed by the load generator at the client and answer the research questions directly; secondary metrics are sampled from the systems under test and explain the primary numbers by exposing resource behaviour. Measuring both from independent vantage points is itself a validity safeguard, since agreement between the client view and the server view corroborates each number~\citep{hasselbring2021benchmarking}. How each metric is collected -- the load generator, the in-process instrumentation, and the host exporter -- is described in \cref{sec:data-tools}; this section defines what is measured and why. \Cref{tab:metrics-summary} lists the full set.

\subsection{Primary Metrics}\label{subsec:primary-metrics}

Primary metrics capture client-observed time behaviour and capacity. Latency is recorded per protocol phase -- catalog request, contract-negotiation initiation, transfer initiation, and data pull -- and end-to-end across the whole transaction, each reported at the median and the 95th and 99th percentiles. Throughput is the rate of completed transactions sustained at a given offered load, and data-plane throughput is recorded separately in megabytes per second to isolate raw transfer capacity from the control-plane handshake.

These three percentiles are used because a latency measurement is a distribution, not a value. The median, written p50, is the value below which half of the observed requests fall, and so describes the typical request. The 95th and 99th percentiles, p95 and p99, are the values below which 95\,\% and 99\,\% of requests fall, and so describe the slowest one in twenty and the slowest one in a hundred. Reporting all three exposes the shape of the distribution instead of a single point on it. This matters because a connector can hold a low median while its tail degrades sharply under load, and the tail governs what a caller actually experiences once many requests are in flight~\citep{dean2013tail}. The arithmetic mean is not used as a headline figure. A small number of very slow requests displaces it, in a way that cannot be distinguished from a uniform slowdown of the whole system. This is a recurring source of misreported performance results~\citep{hoefler2015scientific}.

Two phases of the cycle are asynchronous: the consumer initiates a request and then polls until the contract reaches an agreed state and until an endpoint data reference becomes available. The time to each of these states is therefore measured by polling and is reported as an upper bound within one poll interval; the quantisation this introduces is recorded with each run and revisited in \cref{chap:results-evaluation}. A failure rate -- the fraction of transactions that do not complete -- accompanies every latency figure, since latency is meaningful only over successful transactions. The latency metrics map to the ISO/IEC~25010 time-behaviour sub-characteristic and the throughput metrics to capacity~\citep{iso25010}; the two are read together rather than in isolation, following the relationship between throughput, concurrency, and latency expressed by Little's law~\citep{little1961proof}.

\subsection{Secondary Metrics}\label{subsec:secondary-metrics}

Secondary metrics capture resource utilisation under identical workloads. At the host level, a metrics exporter samples \gls{cpu} busy time and available memory, which establishes whether a measured ceiling belongs to the connector or to the host. At the process level, in-process instrumentation samples \gls{jvm} heap occupancy, \gls{gc} pause activity, and thread count for each connector runtime, together revealing memory pressure, collector overhead, and queuing under load. These map to the ISO/IEC~25010 resource-utilisation sub-characteristic~\citep{iso25010} and are interpreted through the \gls{use} method applied to each connector resource~\citep{gregg2012thinking}. Resource series are aligned to the measurement window so that a latency knee can be read against the CPU or heap behaviour that produced it.

\section{Summary}\label{sec:research-summary}

This chapter establishes the methodological basis for the benchmark. It argues that no single framework spans the path from research goal to credible result. It therefore combines four established methods into a build-then-audit pipeline~\citep{caldiera1994goal, jain1991art, hoefler2015scientific, Ralph2020, iso25010}. The \gls{gqm} framework supplies goal-to-metric traceability. The performance-analysis procedure and the scientific-benchmarking reporting rules supply construction and statistics. The ACM SIGSOFT Empirical Standard and the benchmark quality criteria supply the validity audit, and ISO/IEC~25010 supplies the metric vocabulary. It then decomposes the thesis-level questions into two identity-axis goals, identity enabled and identity disabled, with five recurring research questions per goal. One cross-goal analysis accompanies them: the per-connector identity overhead X1, whose interpretation against the connectors' architectural differences answers TRQ2. It defines the metric set in two tiers: primary, client-observed latency and throughput metrics that answer the questions directly, and secondary, resource-utilisation metrics that explain them, each tied to an ISO/IEC~25010 performance-efficiency facet. One thing this chapter does not settle is how the resulting samples are reduced to an answer. That reduction is a property of the measurement apparatus, not of the research design, and \cref{sec:data-analysis} specifies it alongside the apparatus. \Cref{chap:implementation} realises this design as a concrete deployment, workload, and instrumentation, and \cref{chap:results-evaluation} reports the measurements, with X1 as the headline identity-overhead finding.
