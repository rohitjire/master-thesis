\chapter{Methodology}\label{chap:methodology}

This chapter turns the thesis-level research questions of \cref{sec:research-questions} into a measurable benchmarking design. It selects and combines the methodological frameworks that govern the study, decomposes the research questions into goals, questions, and metrics, and defines the performance metrics that the later chapters collect and interpret.


\section{Research Methodology}\label{sec:research-methodology}

The objective stated in \cref{sec:research-objective} -- a systematic and reproducible performance comparison of four \gls{dsp} connector implementations -- is pursued here through a controlled benchmarking study. \textbf{TRQ1} and \textbf{TRQ2} are deliberately stated above the level of operational metrics; this chapter refines them into measurable questions and indicators using the \gls{gqm} framework~\citep{caldiera1994goal}, which derives every metric from an explicit measurement goal so that no goal is left unmeasured and no metric is collected without a goal to justify it~\citep{minhas2023gqm}.

No single methodology covers the full path from a research goal to a credible result. The study therefore composes several established methods, each filling a gap the others leave: the \gls{gqm} framework for goal-to-metric traceability~\citep{caldiera1994goal}, Jain's performance-analysis procedure for experiment design and statistical analysis~\citep{jain1991art}, the ACM SIGSOFT Empirical Standard for benchmarking as a validity audit~\citep{Ralph2020, empiricalStandardsBenchmarking}, and ISO/IEC~25010 for the performance-efficiency vocabulary that disciplines metric selection~\citep{iso25010}. \Cref{sec:comparing-benchmarks} argues this composition in full; the remainder of the chapter applies it.

Two design commitments shape everything downstream. First, each connector is benchmarked twice -- once with its real identity and trust stack enabled, and once with identity verification mocked -- so that the difference between the two runs isolates the cost of identity. Second, the connectors are measured one at a time on a normalised substrate, because a shared host and a shared monitoring backend would let the load of one connector contaminate the measurements of another. The concrete deployment, the workload scripts, and the per-connector identity mechanisms are described in \cref{chap:implementation}; the threats these choices raise and the limitations of the resulting measurements are discussed in \cref{chap:results-evaluation}, in keeping with the lean scope of this chapter.

The chapter proceeds as follows. \Cref{sec:comparing-benchmarks} surveys the candidate benchmarking methodologies and justifies the combination this study adopts. \Cref{sec:gqm-decomposition} presents the \gls{gqm} decomposition: the two identity-axis goals, the recurring research questions, and the cross-goal analyses of identity overhead and architectural explanation. \Cref{sec:metrics} defines the primary and secondary performance metrics and ties each to an ISO/IEC~25010 quality characteristic. \Cref{sec:research-summary} summarises.


\section{Selecting and Combining Methodologies}\label{sec:comparing-benchmarks}

A benchmark is best understood as a standard measurement tool coupled with an evaluation methodology, not merely a load generator~\citep{kounev2020systems}. Choosing that methodology is itself a design decision, and several mature candidates exist. This section surveys them, groups them by the part of the benchmarking problem they address, and argues that none is individually sufficient for a fair, asynchronous, cross-implementation connector comparison -- which motivates the combined approach this thesis adopts.

\textbf{Goal-oriented measurement.} The \gls{gqm} framework structures measurement top-down: a measurement goal is refined into questions, each of which is answered by metrics, so that every metric is traceable to a goal~\citep{caldiera1994goal}. It remains in active use for structuring empirical comparison~\citep{minhas2023gqm}. Its strength -- traceability -- is also its boundary: \gls{gqm} prescribes neither how to run a fair experiment nor how to analyse the results.

\textbf{Performance analysis and experiment design.} Jain's procedure supplies that missing rigour through a systematic sequence -- from stating goals and system boundaries, through selecting metrics, parameters, and factors, to designing the experiments and analysing them -- together with a catalogue of common mistakes such as omitting warm-up, reporting means instead of percentiles, and using unrepresentative workloads~\citep{jain1991art}. The broader empirical-software-engineering tradition frames such work as a controlled experiment with explicit scoping, planning, execution, analysis, and presentation phases~\citep{wohlin2012experimentation}. Neither, however, derives \emph{which} metrics matter to a given research goal, which is the question \gls{gqm} answers.

\textbf{Benchmarking-specific methodology.} A dedicated benchmarking literature addresses the construction of the benchmark itself. Systems-benchmarking methodology decomposes any benchmark into three elements -- metrics, workloads, and a measurement methodology -- and codifies quality criteria for each~\citep{kounev2020systems}. Cloud-service benchmarking adds a lifecycle view from the client's perspective, foregrounding repeatability, fairness, and understandability for services accessed over a network~\citep{bermbach2017cloud}, which is the situation of a connector measured through its \gls{api}. Guidance on workload construction stresses relevance: a workload is meaningful only if it mirrors behaviour of interest~\citep{kistowski2015benchmark}. The quality of a finished benchmark can in turn be assessed against five established criteria -- relevance, repeatability, fairness, verifiability, and economy~\citep{huppler2009art}.

\textbf{Validity standards.} Whether a finished study counts as a credible benchmark is the domain of empirical standards. The ACM SIGSOFT Empirical Standard for benchmarking enumerates essential attributes -- benchmark justification, workload specification, fairness to all systems under test, replication-sufficient documentation, and stability through repetition~\citep{Ralph2020, empiricalStandardsBenchmarking} -- and the case for treating benchmarking as a first-class empirical instrument in software-engineering research is established independently~\citep{hasselbring2021benchmarking}. These are checklists: they audit a benchmark, but they do not build one.

\textbf{Statistical rigour and reproducibility.} Sound reporting is a recurring failure point. A study of parallel-computing benchmarks found that performance results are frequently reported with too little statistical care to distinguish a real improvement from noise, and prescribed design and reporting rules to correct this~\citep{hoefler2015scientific}. Principles for reproducible performance evaluation in the cloud~\citep{papadopoulos2021reproducible} and repetition discipline for large-scale benchmarks~\citep{armstrong2001large} reinforce the same point: repeat the runs, report distributions rather than point values, and control the environment.

\textbf{Quality vocabulary.} Metric selection finally needs a shared vocabulary. ISO/IEC~25010 defines performance efficiency through three sub-characteristics -- time behaviour, resource utilisation, and capacity -- which map directly onto latency, resource consumption, and throughput~\citep{iso25010}.

Two further candidates were considered and not adopted as the organising spine. A purely hypothesis-driven design fixes the analysis before the exploratory measurements this study still requires, and a full factorial Design of Experiments multiplies the run matrix beyond what a one-connector-at-a-time schedule on a shared host can absorb~\citep{jain1991art}. Both inform the experiment design in \cref{chap:implementation} without dictating the structure of this chapter.

\textbf{The combined method.} The table makes the complementarity explicit. The \gls{gqm} framework is the spine that ties every metric to a research goal, but is silent on execution and credibility~\citep{caldiera1994goal}. Jain's procedure, reinforced by the statistical discipline of scientific benchmarking, supplies the constructive half: factor control, a discarded warm-up, repetition, and percentile rather than mean analysis~\citep{jain1991art, hoefler2015scientific}. The ACM SIGSOFT Empirical Standard and the benchmark quality criteria supply the evaluative half, auditing whether the constructed benchmark is fair, reproducible, and verifiable~\citep{Ralph2020, empiricalStandardsBenchmarking, huppler2009art}. The benchmarking-specific methodologies shape the metric, workload, and measurement choices in between~\citep{kounev2020systems, bermbach2017cloud}, and ISO/IEC~25010 fixes the metric vocabulary~\citep{iso25010}. Construction without audit risks a rigorous but uncredible benchmark; audit without construction is a checklist with nothing to check. Together they form a build-then-audit pipeline: \gls{gqm} states what to measure, Jain builds the experiment, and the empirical standard verifies that the construction clears the bar a credible benchmark must meet.

This discipline is not bureaucratic overhead; it is the precondition for the study's central comparison. The difference between an identity-enabled and an identity-disabled run, defined in \cref{sec:gqm-decomposition}, can be attributed to the identity layer only if every other factor was held constant across the pair -- which is exactly Jain's factor control and the standard's fairness requirement~\citep{jain1991art, Ralph2020}. The combined method is therefore what licenses reading the measured difference as the cost of identity rather than as noise.


\section{Goal--Question--Metric Decomposition}\label{sec:gqm-decomposition}

The study carries two measurement goals that differ along a single axis -- the identity and trust layer -- and asks the same questions under each. Following the \gls{gqm} goal template of object, purpose, focus, viewpoint, and environment~\citep{caldiera1994goal}, the first goal, \textbf{G1} (performance with identity enabled), is:

\begin{description}
    \item[Object] the \gls{dsp} flow of catalog request, contract negotiation, transfer, and data pull;
    \item[Purpose] to characterise and compare;
    \item[Focus] time behaviour, capacity, and resource utilisation -- the performance-efficiency sub-characteristics of ISO/IEC~25010~\citep{iso25010};
    \item[Viewpoint] a connector operator or integrator;
    \item[Environment] four interoperable \gls{dsp} connector implementations -- Tractus-X EDC, Factory-X EDC, the Fraunhofer ISST \gls{edc}, and DSP-Native BaSyx -- each running its full \gls{dcp} identity stack on a normalised substrate.
\end{description}

The second goal, \textbf{G2} (performance with identity disabled), is identical in every clause except the environment: each connector runs with identity verification mocked. Identity disabled means verification is short-circuited rather than removed -- a mock identity service still issues and accepts a trivial token, but the cryptographic work of the \gls{dcp}, namely secure-token exchange, DID-document resolution, and verifiable-credential presentation and verification, no longer runs~\citep{eclipseEdcIdentityHub}. Because the four implementations disagree most on exactly this layer -- ranging from a full \gls{dcp} stack to a non-EDC validation service -- isolating it cleanly is what the rest of the study relies on. The set deliberately spans more than the EDC core: DSP-Native BaSyx is an \gls{aas} application with native \gls{dsp} support rather than an EDC build, so the neutral term \emph{\gls{dsp} connector implementations} is used when all four are meant~\citep{dam2024surveydataspaceconnectorimplementations}.

\textbf{Cross-goal analyses.} Two analyses are produced by contrasting the goals rather than asked within one, which is why they are not numbered as research questions. \textbf{X1}, the identity overhead, is the per-connector, per-metric difference $G_1 - G_2$: for each question above, the identity-disabled value subtracted from the identity-enabled value. This is the headline result -- the directly measured cost of running real identity, in place of the inference to which an indirect study would be limited. \textbf{X2}, the architectural explanation, asks which design differences -- the identity-stack design foremost -- account for the size of each connector's overhead and for the connector-to-connector gaps measured under G1. It answers \textbf{TRQ2}, interpreting the measured deltas and per-hop timing against documented architectural differences~\citep{evaluatingtheperformanceimpactofdatasovereignty, eclipseEdcIdentityHub}.

\textbf{Are these two goals or one?} In strict \gls{gqm} a goal is fixed by its object, purpose, focus, viewpoint, and environment~\citep{caldiera1994goal}; G1 and G2 share all five and differ only in a configuration setting, so they are formally two levels of one experimental factor rather than two goals in the canonical sense. Presenting them as two goals is a deliberate framing choice that makes identity the headline of the study, and it is defended as such rather than as a technicality. The equivalent canonical formulation -- a single characterise-and-compare goal with identity as a crosscutting factor, the same five questions asked at each level -- yields the identical run matrix and the identical X1 and X2 outputs, so the framing is safe to adopt and cheap to retract.

\textbf{From questions to metrics.} \Cref{tab:gqm-tree} completes the decomposition, mapping each question to its metrics, units, and measurement source. Each metric is collected once per goal, so every row yields a pair of values whose difference is X1. The mapping to ISO/IEC~25010 justifies the choice of metrics: the latency series measure time behaviour, throughput and the saturation point measure capacity, and the CPU, heap, and \gls{gc} series measure resource utilisation~\citep{iso25010}. Tail percentiles are reported alongside medians because the mean conceals the tail that dominates user-perceived behaviour at scale~\citep{dean2013tail}.

\section{Performance Metrics}\label{sec:metrics}

The metrics fall into two tiers. Primary metrics are observed by the load generator at the client and answer the research questions directly; secondary metrics are sampled from the systems under test and explain the primary numbers by exposing resource behaviour. Measuring both from independent vantage points is itself a validity safeguard, since agreement between the client view and the server view corroborates each number~\citep{hasselbring2021benchmarking}. How each metric is collected -- the load generator, the in-process instrumentation, and the host exporter -- is described in \cref{sec:data-tools}; this section defines what is measured and why. \Cref{tab:metrics-summary} lists the full set.

\subsection{Primary Metrics}\label{subsec:primary-metrics}

Primary metrics capture client-observed time behaviour and capacity. Latency is recorded per protocol phase -- catalog request, contract-negotiation initiation, transfer initiation, and data pull -- and end-to-end across the whole transaction, each reported at the median and the 95th and 99th percentiles. Throughput is the rate of completed transactions sustained at a given offered load, and data-plane throughput is recorded separately in megabytes per second to isolate raw transfer capacity from the control-plane handshake.

Two phases of the cycle are asynchronous: the consumer initiates a request and then polls until the contract reaches an agreed state and until an endpoint data reference becomes available. The time to each of these states is therefore measured by polling and is reported as an upper bound within one poll interval; the quantisation this introduces is recorded with each run and revisited in \cref{chap:results-evaluation}. A failure rate -- the fraction of transactions that do not complete -- accompanies every latency figure, since latency is meaningful only over successful transactions. The latency metrics map to the ISO/IEC~25010 time-behaviour sub-characteristic and the throughput metrics to capacity~\citep{iso25010}; percentiles rather than means are reported throughout~\citep{dean2013tail}, consistent with the relationship between throughput, concurrency, and latency expressed by Little's law~\citep{little1961proof}.

\subsection{Secondary Metrics}\label{subsec:secondary-metrics}

Secondary metrics capture resource utilisation under identical workloads. At the host level, a metrics exporter samples \gls{cpu} busy time and available memory, which establishes whether a measured ceiling belongs to the connector or to the host. At the process level, in-process instrumentation samples \gls{jvm} heap occupancy, \gls{gc} pause activity, and thread count for each connector runtime, together revealing memory pressure, collector overhead, and queuing under load. These map to the ISO/IEC~25010 resource-utilisation sub-characteristic~\citep{iso25010} and are interpreted through the \gls{use} method applied to each connector resource~\citep{gregg2012thinking}. Resource series are aligned to the measurement window so that a latency knee can be read against the CPU or heap behaviour that produced it.

\section{Summary}\label{sec:research-summary}

This chapter established the methodological basis for the benchmark. It argued that no single framework spans the path from research goal to credible result, and combined four established methods into a build-then-audit pipeline: the \gls{gqm} framework for goal-to-metric traceability, Jain's procedure and the scientific-benchmarking reporting rules for construction and statistics, the ACM SIGSOFT Empirical Standard and the benchmark quality criteria for the validity audit, and ISO/IEC~25010 for the metric vocabulary~\citep{caldiera1994goal, jain1991art, hoefler2015scientific, Ralph2020, iso25010}. It then decomposed the thesis-level questions into two identity-axis goals -- identity enabled and identity disabled -- with five recurring research questions per goal, and two cross-goal analyses: the per-connector identity overhead X1 and its architectural explanation X2. Finally it defined the metric set in two tiers: primary, client-observed latency and throughput metrics that answer the questions directly, and secondary, resource-utilisation metrics that explain them, each tied to an ISO/IEC~25010 performance-efficiency facet. \Cref{chap:implementation} realises this design as a concrete deployment, workload, and instrumentation; \cref{chap:results-evaluation} reports the measurements, with X1 as the headline identity-overhead finding.
