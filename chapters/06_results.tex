\chapter{Results and Evaluation}\label{chap:results-evaluation}

This chapter reports the measurements that answer the research questions of \cref{sec:gqm-decomposition}. \Crefrange{sec:rq1}{sec:rq5} answer the five recurring questions, each under both identity goals, \cref{sec:x1-overhead} draws the cross-goal identity overhead X1 together across them, and \cref{sec:results-validity} states the threats to the validity of both.

Every connector was measured twice under conditions identical in every respect but one. In the identity-enabled arm, written $G_1$, the connector runs its real \gls{dcp} identity stack; in the identity-disabled arm, $G_2$, verification is short-circuited by a mock that issues and accepts a trivial token (\cref{subsec:mock-arm}). One connector in one of those two states is an \emph{arm}, so the three connectors give six arms, and every table below reports both arms of a connector together. X1 is the difference between them on the same metric: the measured cost of running real identity, and the one quantity no single run produces.

The \emph{operating point} is the fixed load at which the connectors are compared head to head -- one transaction per second, held for five minutes and offered identically to every arm (\cref{subsec:scenarios}). \Cref{sec:rq1,sec:rq4} report measurements taken there. The remaining sections each vary one thing. \Cref{sec:rq2} raises the offered load in rungs until delivery breaks down, \cref{sec:rq3} adds a thirty-minute endurance run, and \cref{sec:rq5} varies catalog size instead of load. Each says so where it departs from the operating point.

Every value reported here is a median across the repetitions of a run, with the smallest and largest of those repetitions given beside it as the measure of run-to-run repeatability. $\Delta$ is the difference between the identity-enabled ($G_1$) and identity-disabled ($G_2$) medians of a metric, $\tilde{x}_{G_1}-\tilde{x}_{G_2}$. It is reported together with their ratio on the $G_1$ row, so that both arms of a connector appear in the same table. Heap growth in the endurance runs is the one quantity reported differently, as a least-squares slope in MB/h. \Cref{subsec:analysis-statistics} sets out why these statistics and no others, and the rule by which a run is admitted or excluded. Both apply uniformly to every connector and arm, with no per-connector special-casing.

\textbf{Throughput does not distinguish the connectors at the operating point.} Every arm delivered the offered load: completed transactions range from 0.915 to 0.950\,tx/s against an offered 1\,tx/s, a spread of under four per cent across all six arms. It is reported for completeness, not as a result. Capacity is the subject of \cref{sec:rq2}.

\textbf{Latency does distinguish them.} Under $G_1$ the two \gls{edc} connectors are indistinguishable from one another: 4672.5\,ms for the Fraunhofer ISST \gls{edc} against 4649.0\,ms for Factory-X, a difference of 23.5\,ms, or 0.5\,\%. DSP-Native BaSyx completes the same transaction in 2884.5\,ms, some 38\,\% faster. Under $G_2$ the ordering is preserved but the separation widens: 4198.0\,ms and 4416.0\,ms for the \gls{edc} pair against 814.0\,ms for BaSyx, which is then roughly one fifth of either.

\textbf{Five repetitions bound the resolution of every comparison.} Run-to-run variation of the median, expressed as the relative range, is between 0.6\,\% and 9.2\,\%, and it is consistently smaller in the identity-disabled arms (0.6--2.6\,\%) than in the identity-enabled ones (4.8--9.2\,\%). Every comparison drawn from this table is bounded by those figures: the 23.5\,ms separating the two \gls{edc} connectors under $G_1$ is far below the 4.8--5.4\,\% variation of either, and is not interpreted as a difference. The ratio of mean to median lies between 0.97 and 1.01 in all six arms. The distributions are therefore close to symmetric at this load, and the choice of median over mean changes nothing here. That choice earns its place under load, not at the operating point (\cref{subsec:analysis-statistics}).

\Cref{fig:rq1-payload} reports data-plane capacity against payload size. The \texttt{payload-sweep} scenario holds four concurrent users against each of five payload sizes from 1\,KB to 100\,MB for one minute apiece. It negotiates once and then repeats only the transfer, so its unit of observation is a pull, not a transaction. DSP-Native BaSyx collapses control and data plane into one process and has no separate data plane to exercise, so it is absent from the figure by construction (\cref{sec:sut}).

\textbf{Data-plane throughput rises monotonically with payload size and saturates at the largest transfers.} For the Fraunhofer ISST \gls{edc} it climbs from 0.27\,MB/s at 1\,KB to 515.1\,MB/s at 100\,MB, and for Factory-X from 0.26\,MB/s to 458.2\,MB/s. Per-request overhead dominates small payloads, not transfer time. A 1\,KB pull takes 3.7--3.9\,ms and a 100\,KB pull 4.0--4.3\,ms, so a hundredfold increase in payload costs under ten per cent in time. Beyond 1\,MB the pull duration grows in proportion to size.

\textbf{The identity arms are indistinguishable throughout.} Across all five sizes and both connectors the largest difference in pull latency between $G_1$ and $G_2$ is 7.5\,ms, on the 100\,MB transfer. The achieved rates differ by at most 3.7\,\%, which is within the run-to-run variation reported in \cref{tab:rq1-steady}. The verified byte counts confirm that each run transferred the asset its label names, ruling out a substituted asset as an explanation for the agreement.

\textbf{Answer to RQ1.} At the operating point throughput is the same in every arm and carries no information. Latency separates the connectors. With identity enabled the two \gls{edc} connectors are indistinguishable from one another, and DSP-Native BaSyx completes the same transaction in roughly three fifths of their time. The data plane behaves alike in all arms across every payload size, so the whole of the difference sits in the control plane.

\section{RQ2 --- Behaviour under Rising Load and Concurrency}\label{sec:rq2}

% G_k.RQ2: latency and success rate as offered load and concurrency rise -- the
% saturation knee and the onset of failure. Sources: rq2-saturation.csv,
% rq2-concurrency.csv.

\Cref{fig:rq2-saturation} reports the open-model saturation ladder for both goals. The \texttt{saturation-open} scenario raises the offered arrival rate through seven rungs (1, 1.5, 2.5, 4, 6.5, 10 and 12\,tx/s), holding each for ninety seconds. It was repeated three times per arm (\cref{subsec:scenarios}). The controlled variable is offered load, not client population. This avoids the coordinated-omission bias to which a closed model is subject when the server slows~\citep{schroeder2006open}. The offered rate of each rung is the mean arrival rate over that stage, not its nominal target, because the load generator interpolates linearly towards the target across the stage. Comparing delivery against the nominal value understates it on every ramped rung~\citep{k6docs}.

\textbf{Delivery tracks the offered rate to 2.5\,tx/s.} All six arms deliver between 89\,\% and 99\,\% of a mean offered rate through the first three rungs. The ladder separates them at the fourth rung, an offered mean of 4\,tx/s. There the two BaSyx arms and both Fraunhofer ISST arms still deliver 83--87\,\%, whereas Factory-X falls to 50--54\,\% in both arms. It delivers 2.0--2.2\,tx/s while 4\,tx/s is offered, which is less than it delivered at the preceding rung. Factory-X identity-enabled is the only arm to breach the 90\,\% criterion earlier, at the third rung, with 89\,\%.

\textbf{Beyond the fourth rung the arms diverge in shape as well as level.} The Fraunhofer ISST \gls{edc} plateaus across the middle of the ladder. Delivered throughput holds at 3.9--4.1\,tx/s in both arms at an offered 6.5 and 10\,tx/s, and rises only to 4.6 and 5.2\,tx/s at the top rung of 12\,tx/s. By that point each arm converts under half of what is offered. DSP-Native BaSyx continues to climb, reaching 6.0\,tx/s identity-disabled and 5.9\,tx/s identity-enabled at the top rung. Its delivery ratio falls smoothly, not abruptly. Factory-X shows neither pattern cleanly. Its delivered rate dips at the fourth rung before recovering to 4.6--4.8\,tx/s at an offered 10\,tx/s. Its identity-enabled arm returned no measurement at the top rung, so that point is absent from the figure. \Cref{sec:results-validity} records that omission.

\textbf{Delivery counts completions, not successes.} These figures therefore include transactions abandoned at the poll deadline. The proportion so abandoned over the whole ladder is 22--34\,\% for the Fraunhofer ISST and BaSyx arms and 50\,\% for both Factory-X arms.

\Cref{fig:rq2-concurrency} reports the closed-model ladder as an independent check on the same ceiling. The \texttt{concurrency-closed} scenario replaces the arrival rate with a fixed client population, stepping through 5, 10, 20 and 50 concurrent users at ninety seconds each. It ran once per arm. Under a closed model a saturated server shows throughput flattening while residence time grows in proportion to the client population, which is the form Little's law takes at saturation~\citep{little1961proof}. Agreement between the two workload models is what makes an observed ceiling a property of the connector rather than of one model.

\textbf{The closed model agrees with the open one on which arms are saturated.} DSP-Native BaSyx identity-disabled scales close to linearly -- 3.05, 8.51, 17.49 and 39.49\,tx/s at 5, 10, 20 and 50 virtual users -- while its implied residence time stays flat between 0.82 and 0.90\,s. A client population that grows tenfold without lengthening the transaction is the signature of a server that is not the constraint.

\textbf{Every other arm shows the opposite.} DSP-Native BaSyx identity-enabled delivers 0.89, 1.31, 2.39 and 3.83\,tx/s over the same ladder, while implied residence time rises from 2.82 to 9.13\,s. The Fraunhofer ISST identity-disabled arm delivers 0.51, 1.24 and 2.28\,tx/s, with residence time rising from 4.89 to 6.58\,s. Throughput growing more slowly than the client population, with residence time absorbing the difference, is the form Little's law takes at saturation~\citep{little1961proof}.

\textbf{Answer to RQ2.} All six arms convert offered load into completed transactions up to 2.5\,tx/s. Beyond that they separate: Factory-X delivers about half of what is offered and rises no further, the Fraunhofer ISST \gls{edc} plateaus near 4\,tx/s, and DSP-Native BaSyx continues to climb at every rung tested. The closed-model ladder agrees with the open one on which arms are saturated, so the ordering is a property of the connectors and not of the load model.

\section{RQ3 --- Resource Consumption}\label{sec:rq3}

% G_k.RQ3: CPU, JVM heap, GC, threads under identical workloads, plus the host
% ceiling. Sources: rq3-resources.csv, rq3-drift.csv.

\Cref{tab:rq3-resources} reports host and \gls{jvm} resource consumption under the same workloads. The processor, heap and collector figures are sampled during the \texttt{steady} and \texttt{saturation-open} runs already reported in \cref{sec:rq1,sec:rq2}, so no additional load was applied to obtain them. Peaks are reported alongside medians because of the question this data must answer: was an observed throughput ceiling imposed by the host or by the connector? A connector that saturates while the host retains headroom is limited by its own structure. That is an architectural finding, not a hardware one~\citep{gregg2012thinking}.

\textbf{Host \gls{cpu} never approached exhaustion.} Peak busy time across the measured windows is 48.1\,\% for the Fraunhofer ISST identity-enabled arm, 42.7\,\% for Factory-X and 31.6\,\% for BaSyx, with medians between 2.3\,\% and 16.9\,\%. Since every arm reached its delivered-throughput ceiling within these runs (\cref{sec:rq2}), the ceiling is not the host: at the point where each connector stopped converting offered load into completed transactions, at least half of the machine remained idle.

\textbf{The connectors differ in memory and collector behaviour rather than in processor demand.} Peak heap is 725.2\,MB for Factory-X, 665.9\,MB for BaSyx identity-enabled and 508.3\,MB for the Fraunhofer ISST \gls{edc}. Garbage-collection activity separates them more sharply: aggregated across instrumented runtimes it peaks at 0.605\,s/s for Factory-X and 0.320\,s/s for the Fraunhofer ISST \gls{edc}, against 0.011\,s/s for BaSyx -- a difference of nearly two orders of magnitude.

\textbf{The identity layer's effect on memory is confined to one connector.} Peak heap differs between arms by 1.8\,\% for the Fraunhofer ISST \gls{edc} and 0.9\,\% for Factory-X, both within run-to-run variation, whereas BaSyx holds 665.9\,MB identity-enabled against 323.8\,MB identity-disabled, a factor of 2.06.

\textbf{No service grows fast enough to threaten its container limit.} Long-run stability was measured with the \texttt{soak} scenario, a gentle 0.5\,tx/s held for thirty minutes and run once per arm, with heap
occupancy sampled every five seconds for each instrumented runtime and a least-squares
trend fitted to each series. No service in any arm grows fast enough to threaten its
container limit. The fastest-growing series is the Fraunhofer ISST \gls{edc} consumer
control plane under $G_1$ at 19.2\,MB/h, followed by the DSP-Native BaSyx provider at
10.3\,MB/h. The two Factory-X arms reach only 7.0 and 1.8\,MB/h, and every series
in the BaSyx identity-disabled arm is flat or falling. Against heaps bounded at 768\,MB
and observed occupancies between 40 and 190\,MB, none of these rates would exhaust the
available memory during the observation window or soon after it.

\textbf{The window bounds the claim rather than the measurement.} Thirty minutes is sufficient to
show that no component grows catastrophically, and insufficient to exclude a slow leak;
\cref{sec:conclusion-limitations} records that limit.

\textbf{Answer to RQ3.} Processor demand neither separates the connectors nor limits them. Host \gls{cpu} peaked below half in every arm. What separates them is memory and collector behaviour, where garbage-collection activity differs by nearly two orders of magnitude between the \gls{edc} connectors and BaSyx. No service in any arm grows fast enough to exhaust its heap within the observation window.

\section{RQ4 --- The Asynchronous Cycle, Decomposed by Phase}\label{sec:rq4}

\Cref{tab:rq4-phases} decomposes the transaction into the phases of the \gls{dsp} cycle. The phase durations come from the same \texttt{steady} runs as \cref{sec:rq1}, so the end-to-end figure decomposed here is the one reported there. Reporting both goals per phase localises the identity cost to a specific point in the protocol, which is the evidence the architectural interpretation of \cref{sec:x1-overhead} rests on.

\textbf{The two asynchronous waits dominate every arm of every connector.} Time-to-agreed accounts for 54.8--61.7\,\% of the end-to-end median and time-to-\gls{edr} for a further 35.3--38.3\,\%; together they are between 93 and 97\,\% of the transaction. The synchronous steps are negligible by comparison: negotiation initiation, transfer initiation and the data pull together account for 1.0--1.7\,\%, and the catalog request for 1.9--2.3\,\%. This holds identically for the two \gls{edc} connectors and for the architecturally different BaSyx provider.

\textbf{Where the identity layer's cost falls differs sharply between connectors.} On the two \gls{edc} connectors it concentrates in the catalog request. That request rises from 8.3 to 101.9\,ms on the Fraunhofer ISST \gls{edc} (a factor of 12.2) and from 9.6 to 86.8\,ms on Factory-X (a factor of 9.1), while the two asynchronous waits change by 0.2--10\,\%. On BaSyx the pattern is inverted: the catalog request rises by 47.9\,ms, but time-to-agreed rises from 254 to 1781\,ms and time-to-\gls{edr} from 253 to 1017\,ms, so the cost falls almost entirely inside the asynchronous waits.

\textbf{One phase is consistently faster with identity enabled than without it.} Negotiation initiation takes 6.2\,ms against 50.1\,ms on the Fraunhofer ISST \gls{edc} and 7.0\,ms against 45.8\,ms on Factory-X -- ratios of 0.12 and 0.15 -- and 6.8\,ms against 10.4\,ms on BaSyx. The measurement is reported as observed; \cref{sec:x1-overhead} considers what would account for it.

\textbf{The phase budget reconciles with the end-to-end median.} The residuals are $-1$\,ms for BaSyx, $-77$\,ms for the Fraunhofer ISST \gls{edc} and $+41$\,ms for Factory-X. Each is under two per cent of the end-to-end figure, so the shares above may be read as a time budget and not only as an ordering. The polling that produced these boundaries cost 10--13 negotiation polls and 6--10 \gls{edr} polls per transaction. This is the observer effect the figures must be read net of, and \cref{sec:results-validity} quantifies it directly.

\textbf{Answer to RQ4.} The transaction is almost entirely waiting. Between 93 and 97\,\% of it is spent waiting for the two asynchronous state machines to reach their terminal states, in every arm of every connector. The synchronous steps together account for one to two per cent. Identity's cost lands in different places: in the catalog request on the two \gls{edc} connectors, and inside the asynchronous waits on BaSyx. \Cref{sec:x1-overhead} builds its architectural reading on that observation.

\textbf{Catalog latency is insensitive to catalog size until the largest level.} On the Fraunhofer ISST \gls{edc} the identity-enabled median moves from 426.2\,ms at one asset to 415.0 and 418.3\,ms at ten and one hundred, then to 697.0\,ms at one thousand. Factory-X follows the same shape, from 654.5 through 655.1 and 679.6 to 1153.6\,ms. Over a thousandfold increase in catalog size, request latency grows by a factor of 1.64 and 1.76 respectively.

\textbf{The identity cost across that sweep is close to constant in absolute terms.} For the Fraunhofer ISST \gls{edc} it is 135.8, 124.0, 105.8 and 131.4\,ms at the four sizes, and for Factory-X 92.0, 108.8, 72.3 and 36.1\,ms. Because the underlying latency grows while the difference does not, the same cost expressed as a ratio falls with catalog size: from 1.47 to 1.23 for the Fraunhofer ISST \gls{edc} and from 1.16 to 1.03 for Factory-X.

\textbf{Answer to RQ5.} Catalog-request latency is insensitive to catalog size across three of the four levels and rises only at the largest, by a factor of 1.64 and 1.76 over a thousandfold increase in advertised assets. The identity cost across the same sweep is near-constant in absolute terms, so its relative weight falls as the catalog grows.

Measured end to end, the identity layer costs the Fraunhofer ISST \gls{edc} 474.5\,ms per
transaction (a factor of 1.11) and Factory-X 233.0\,ms (1.05). Sustained throughput is
unaffected in both: the arms deliver the same rate to within one per cent, because at an
operating point below saturation the offered rate, not the connector, determines
delivery. The tail moves with the median rather than faster than it: p95 rises by
388.8\,ms and 291.0\,ms respectively. Identity therefore shifts the distribution instead of
lengthening its tail disproportionately.

Measured on the provider alone the same cost is 30.0\,ms (1.15) and 41.3\,ms (1.22), and
for DSP-Native BaSyx 44.7\,ms (2.90). The load control holds in all three cases. The
provider served the same request rate in both arms to within one per cent. A higher
latency at equal load therefore isolates the difference to the work performed per request,
which is the credential verification itself.

\textbf{The cost is fixed per request, not proportional to the work.} The catalog sweep
separates the two possibilities, because it varies the work while holding the identity
operation constant. Across a thousandfold increase in catalog size the difference stays
within a narrow band: 135.8, 124.0, 105.8 and 131.4\,ms for the Fraunhofer ISST
\gls{edc}, and 92.0, 108.8, 72.3 and 36.1\,ms for Factory-X. The underlying
latency meanwhile grows by factors of 1.64 and 1.76. Expressed as a ratio the same cost therefore
falls as the catalog grows, from 1.47 to 1.23 and from 1.16 to 1.03. A verification
performed once per request, independent of how much the request returns, produces exactly
this signature. Its practical consequence is that the relative penalty of running real
identity diminishes as the workload per request increases.

\textbf{The cost is confined to the control plane.} \Cref{fig:rq1-payload} shows the four
data-plane series lying almost on top of one another across five orders of magnitude, with
the largest difference in pull latency being 7.5\,ms on a 100\,MB transfer. Identity is
paid once per transaction and nothing per byte transferred, which is consistent with a
design that establishes trust before the transfer begins and then hands the data plane a
token rather than re-verifying during it~\citep{dcpSpec}.

\textbf{Where the cost lands differs by architecture, and this is the observation that
most constrains an explanation of the connector-to-connector differences.} On both
\gls{edc} connectors the increase concentrates almost entirely in the catalog request,
which rises by factors of 12.2 and 9.1, while the two asynchronous waits change by between
zero and ten per cent. On DSP-Native BaSyx the pattern inverts: the catalog request rises
by 47.9\,ms, but time-to-agreed rises by a factor of 7.0 and time-to-\gls{edr} by 4.0, so
the cost falls inside the asynchronous state machine rather than ahead of it. The
\gls{edc} pattern is what a design that verifies a presentation once, at the first
protected request of an exchange, and then proceeds against an established context would
produce; the BaSyx pattern is what per-message validation inside the protocol handlers
would produce. Both readings are consistent with the respective documented
designs~\citep{dcpSpec, dspProtocolLib}, and the traces of \cref{subsec:tracing} are the
evidence that would confirm or refute them directly. % TODO: needs source -- confirm against the EDC DCP implementation whether verification is per-exchange or per-message.

One measurement resists this account. Negotiation initiation is consistently \emph{faster}
with identity enabled than without it -- 6.2\,ms against 50.1\,ms on the Fraunhofer ISST
\gls{edc}, 7.0\,ms against 45.8\,ms on Factory-X, and 6.8\,ms against 10.4\,ms on BaSyx.
The effect is large, consistent across connectors and outside run-to-run variation, so it
is not noise. It is reported here as observed rather than explained. The identity-disabled
arms substitute a mock identity service for the real one, and a mock that answers
synchronously on the request path could plausibly cost more at that step than a real
implementation that has already established its context. This campaign did not measure
the mock's internal behaviour, however, and cannot distinguish that explanation from
alternatives.

\textbf{The BaSyx figure is not comparable with the other two.} Its identity-disabled arm
replaces the \gls{edc} consumer with a callback sink and drives the provider with a
different load generator (\cref{subsec:mock-arm}), so the client-side difference of
2070.5\,ms mixes the identity layer with an entirely different consumer stack. The
provider-side figure of 44.7\,ms is the one that may be compared, and it is measured on
the same component by the same agent in both arms. The ratio of 2.90 remains the largest
of the three connectors, but the absolute cost -- 44.7\,ms against 30.0 and 41.3\,ms --
places all three connectors within the same order of magnitude once the comparison is made
on equal terms.

\section{Threats to Validity}\label{sec:results-validity}

% Chapters 2, 3, 4 and 5 forward-reference this section; keep the label.

Every measurement in this chapter has limits, and this section states them: what the
numbers actually measure, and what could have biased the comparison between the two arms
of a connector. How far the results carry beyond this setup, and what may be concluded
from them, are stated in \cref{sec:conclusion-limitations}.

\subsection{What the Numbers Measure}\label{subsec:tv-construct}

\textbf{Polling rounds every phase up.} The harness detects a state change by asking
repeatedly, every 250\,ms, so a phase that ends between two questions is recorded as
ending at the later one. Each phase duration is therefore an overestimate, by up to one
interval.

The size of that error was measured rather than assumed. Repeating the operating point at
a 1000\,ms interval lengthened the reported end-to-end median by 11--15\,\% on the
\gls{edc} connectors -- close to what half an interval per polled phase predicts. At the
250\,ms interval used throughout, the overstatement is a few hundred milliseconds on a
transaction of four to five seconds. It applies equally to every arm, so it inflates the
absolute latencies without biasing any comparison between them.

\textbf{Failed transactions make latency look better than it was.} A transaction still
unfinished after 30\,s is abandoned, and contributes that 30\,s rather than however long
it would really have taken. Percentiles from a run with failures are therefore lower
bounds. The operating-point figures in \cref{sec:rq1} come from runs with no failures at
all; the ladder in \cref{sec:rq2} does contain them, which is why it is read as evidence
of \emph{where} delivery breaks down and not as a latency measurement.

\textbf{Measuring costs the connector something.} About sixty per cent of the requests
each connector answered at the operating point were the harness asking whether a phase had
finished. That load falls on the consumer, not the provider. The provider-side figures in
\cref{tab:x1-provider} are consequently the least disturbed by the act of measuring, which
is a further reason to prefer them when comparing connectors.

\subsection{What Could Have Biased the Comparison}\label{subsec:tv-internal}

\textbf{Some scenarios ran once.} The operating point was repeated five times per arm and
the load ladder three times, but the concurrency ladder and the endurance run once each.
Those two carry no variability estimate, and their figures are single observations.

\textbf{One ladder point is missing.} The Factory-X identity-enabled arm returned no
delivered-rate value at the top rung of the saturation ladder, so its series in
\cref{fig:rq2-saturation} ends at an offered 10\,tx/s. That rung lies well beyond the
point at which the arm stopped tracking offered load, so what the omission costs is the
shape of its curve past saturation rather than the saturation point itself, which
\cref{sec:rq2} locates at the fourth rung.

\textbf{Scenarios always ran in the same order.} Randomising would have averaged out any
carry-over between them, but it would also have let a connector's result depend on which
scenario happened to precede it, which is the larger risk (\cref{subsec:orchestration}).
The age of the running stack is recorded with every run, so a carry-over effect can be
tested for afterwards rather than assumed absent.

\textbf{Three differences between arms were not eliminated.} The DSP-Native BaSyx
identity-disabled arm differs from its partner in the consumer stack and the load driver
as well as in the identity layer, which is why its end-to-end overhead is excluded from
cross-connector comparison and the provider-side figure used instead. The endurance runs
were repeated after a load-generator configuration defect was corrected, so they carry a
different driver fingerprint from the rest. And the instrumentation agent has a cost that
was not measured separately here; because the same agent runs everywhere, it is a constant
of the comparison rather than something that varies by connector.

\textbf{Server errors occurred, and were rare.} They account for 0.18\,\% of requests on
the BaSyx identity-enabled consumer, up to 0.04\,\% on Factory-X, and none at all on the
Fraunhofer ISST \gls{edc} across fifty runs. Almost all occur in the ramped scenarios,
past the point where delivery had already broken down.
