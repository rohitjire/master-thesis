\chapter{Introduction}\label{chap:introduction}

\section{Motivation}\label{sec:motivation}

Industrial value chains increasingly depend on data exchanged across organisational boundaries. Predictive maintenance pulls sensor telemetry from suppliers, carbon-footprint reporting aggregates emissions data from upstream tiers, and circular-economy use cases trace materials across the lifetime of a product. In each of these scenarios the participants are competitors as much as collaborators, and the data they exchange is commercially sensitive. The European Union has identified this tension as a strategic concern and has called for a single market for data in which actors retain sovereignty over the data they share \citep{europeancommission2020}. The dominant cloud-centric pattern, in which data is consolidated into a small number of hyperscaler platforms, is increasingly seen as incompatible with this goal \citep{designingdataspace}.

Dataspaces have emerged as the architectural response. A dataspace is a federation of independent participants that exchange data peer-to-peer under shared rules for identity, usage policy, and contract negotiation, without consolidating storage in a central authority \citep{IDS4.0}. The International Data Spaces (IDS) initiative and the Gaia-X association have produced the conceptual and governance scaffolding for this model \citep{IDS4.0, GaiaXTrustFramework2024}, and the Eclipse Dataspace Working Group has standardised the wire-level interactions in the \gls{dsp} \citep{datapsaceProtocol2025}. Industrial initiatives such as Catena-X in the automotive sector and Factory-X / Manufacturing-X in discrete manufacturing have moved these specifications from reference architecture to production-track deployment \citep{catenaxDocumentation}.

The component that turns these specifications into running software is the connector. Every cross-organisation data exchange in a dataspace passes through a pair of connectors: one on the provider side, exposing a catalog and enforcing usage policy, and one on the consumer side, requesting access and orchestrating the transfer \citep{dam2024surveydataspaceconnectorimplementations}. The \gls{edc}, maintained by the Eclipse Foundation, has become the de-facto reference implementation and is the basis of multiple downstream variants tailored to specific consortia and use cases \citep{dam2024surveydataspaceconnectorimplementations}.

Despite this operational centrality, the performance of dataspace connectors remains largely unmeasured. Public documentation describes what each connector does, not how fast it does it, and the small number of empirical studies in the literature address either a single connector or a single design dimension \citep{evaluatingtheperformanceimpactofdatasovereignty, dam2024surveydataspaceconnectorimplementations}. Practitioners planning a production rollout, and researchers reasoning about the scalability of the dataspace model, therefore work without a quantitative baseline.


\section{Problem Statement}\label{sec:problem-statement}

The dataspace connector ecosystem is heterogeneous. Several \gls{edc}-derived implementations exist in parallel. For this thesis we focus on four implementations -- Tractus-X EDC, Factory-X EDC, the DSP-Native BaSyx connector, and the Fraunhofer ISST EDC build -- and although all four implement the \gls{dsp}, they differ in their identity layer, transfer-process mechanics, persistence choices, and extension model. These differences are documented at the level of features and supported protocols, but their performance consequences are not.

The existing comparative literature reinforces this gap. The most comprehensive cross-implementation survey to date frames its analysis as feature- and architecture-centric and explicitly identifies performance evaluation as out of scope and as future work \citep{dam2024surveydataspaceconnectorimplementations}. A complementary, performance-oriented study compares two non-\gls{edc} connector implementations -- the Dataspace Connector and the Prometheus-X Dataspace Connector -- against cloud-native data-sharing services, isolating the single dimension of data-sovereignty overhead and leaving the cross-implementation comparison of \gls{edc}-derived, \gls{dsp}-conformant connectors open \citep{evaluatingtheperformanceimpactofdatasovereignty}.

A second, methodological gap compounds the first. The \gls{dsp} negotiation and transfer cycle is asynchronous, catalog request, contract offer, contract agreement, transfer-process initiation, and data plane handover proceed as a sequence of stateful exchanges rather than as a single request-response pair. Classical benchmarking methodology for synchronous web services does not directly accommodate this control-plane / data-plane split, and the benchmarking-as-empirical-standard literature has not yet produced a reusable instrument for the dataspace domain \citep{hasselbring2021benchmarking, Ralph2020}.

\section{Research Objective}\label{sec:research-objective}

The objective of this thesis is to design, execute, and analyse a systematic and reproducible performance benchmark of four production-track dataspace connector implementations -- Tractus-X EDC, Factory-X EDC, DSP-Native BaSyx, and the Fraunhofer ISST EDC -- under controlled, dataspace-representative workloads. The benchmark targets four performance dimensions: end-to-end latency, sustained throughput, scalability under concurrent load, and resource consumption (\gls{cpu} and memory). Out of scope are security audit, full protocol-compliance testing, and feature-completeness review; these are addressed elsewhere in the literature \citep{dam2024surveydataspaceconnectorimplementations}. Methodologically the work is anchored in the \gls{gqm} framework, follows the empirical standards for software engineering research, treats benchmarking as a first-class empirical instrument, and applies established measurement and statistical-analysis conventions \citep{caldiera1994goal, Ralph2020, hasselbring2021benchmarking, jain1990art}.


\section{Research Questions}\label{sec:research-questions}

Three thesis-level research questions structure the investigation. They are deliberately stated at a level above the operational metrics; \Cref{chap:methodology} decomposes them into measurable sub-questions and indicators via the \gls{gqm} framework.

\begin{description}
    \item[\textbf{TRQ1}] How do current production-track \gls{edc} implementations compare in end-to-end performance under realistic dataspace data-exchange workloads?
    \item[\textbf{TRQ2}] Which architectural and implementation-level factors most strongly drive the observed performance differences across these implementations?
\end{description}

\textbf{TRQ1} addresses the empirical \emph{what}: the latencies, throughputs, scalability curves, and resource footprints that practitioners require for capacity planning. \textbf{TRQ2} addresses the explanatory \emph{why}: linking measured differences back to identity-layer choices, transfer-process mechanics, and extension models documented for each connector. The two questions are interdependent: without \textbf{TRQ1} the performance differences are unknown, and without \textbf{TRQ2} the implications of those differences for design and deployment decisions remain opaque. Together they provide a comprehensive picture of the current state of dataspace connector performance and its architectural drivers.
