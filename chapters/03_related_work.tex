\chapter{Related Work}\label{chap:related-work}

\Cref{chap:background} establishes \emph{what} performance means for a dataspace connector and \emph{how} a sound benchmark is constructed; this chapter surveys the studies that have applied those ideas~--~in adjacent domains and to dataspace connectors specifically~--~and identifies the gap this thesis addresses. \Cref{sec:rw-data-exchange} reviews comparative benchmarks of competing data-exchange and data-serving systems; \Cref{sec:rw-connector-studies} examines the connector-specific literature; and \Cref{sec:gap-analysis} consolidates both into the gap that motivates \textbf{TRQ1} and \textbf{TRQ2} (\cref{sec:research-questions}).


\section{Benchmarks for Data Exchange Systems}\label{sec:rw-data-exchange}

Comparing architecturally different systems that implement a common interface is a recurring problem in systems research, and benchmarking is its established empirical answer~\citep{hasselbring2021benchmarking}. The benchmarking fundamentals of \Cref{sec:benchmarking-fundamentals} are most clearly illustrated by the comparative benchmarks of adjacent domains, from which this thesis inherits its design template even though none of their workloads transfer directly to a federated data-exchange protocol.

\paragraph{Data-serving systems.} The Yahoo! Cloud Serving Benchmark (YCSB) is the canonical instrument for comparing key--value and cloud-serving stores~\citep{cooper2010ycsb}. Its design contributes two ideas this thesis reuses. First, it separates an extensible workload generator from a fixed set of core workloads~--~read-heavy, update-heavy, read-modify-write, and range-scan mixes~--~so that systems with very different internals are exercised through one identical interface~\citep{cooper2010ycsb}. Second, it reports performance not as a single figure but as latency-versus-throughput curves and as scaling behaviour under growing load~\citep{cooper2010ycsb}, the distribution-aware reporting that \Cref{subsec:performance-metrics} adopts. YCSB targets storage systems rather than a multi-party exchange protocol, so its workloads do not apply to a dataspace connector; the transferable contribution is the \emph{abstraction}~--~a common workload that exercises heterogeneous systems identically.

\paragraph{Messaging middleware.} The closest structural analogue to this thesis is \emph{Engineering and Experimentally Benchmarking Open Source MQTT Broker Implementations}, which compares five competing implementations~--~Mosquitto, EMQX, RabbitMQ, VerneMQ, and HiveMQ~--~of a single standardised messaging protocol~\citep{dizdarevic2023mqttbrokers}. The study measures response-time overhead across payload sizes and degraded network conditions (latency, packet loss, jitter) on both AMD64 and ARM64 hosts, using an automated, containerised, and fully reproducible deployment~\citep{dizdarevic2023mqttbrokers}. Two findings carry over. The performance of implementations of one protocol varies materially with implementation choice, payload, and environment~\citep{dizdarevic2023mqttbrokers}~--~the premise that makes a cross-implementation connector comparison worthwhile~--~and an automated, containerised harness is the practical vehicle for a fair comparison. The difference from the present work lies in the unit of work: MQTT is a near-synchronous publish/subscribe exchange, whereas a \gls{dsp} interaction is the asynchronous, multi-message negotiation-and-transfer cycle of \Cref{sec:dsp}, which complicates both workload definition and latency attribution.

\paragraph{Microservice systems and reproducibility.} A dataspace connector is not a monolith but a distributed system of cooperating services~--~control plane, data plane, identity, and catalog (\cref{subsec:edc}). DeathStarBench, a benchmark suite of representative microservice applications, shows that in such systems inter-service network processing grows to a large share of execution time and shifts the resource bottleneck away from where a single-tier analysis would place it~\citep{gan2019deathstarbench}. This is a direct caution for connector measurement: end-to-end latency must be attributed across cooperating services rather than to any one runtime, as \Cref{subsec:performance-metrics} requires. Comparability across studies is itself a methodological target: \emph{Methodological Principles for Reproducible Performance Evaluation in Cloud Computing} sets out eight principles that combine best practice from neighbouring fields with cloud-specific concerns and an explicit time--accuracy trade-off~\citep{papadopoulos2021reproducible}. This thesis treats reproducibility as a first-class requirement on that basis (\cref{sec:reproducibility}).

These benchmarks demonstrate that comparative, implementation-level performance evaluation is a mature method in storage, messaging, and microservice systems. No comparable instrument yet exists for dataspace connectors, which \Cref{sec:rw-connector-studies} examines next.


\section{Performance Studies on Dataspace Connectors}\label{sec:rw-connector-studies}

The connector-specific literature is substantial on features, compliance, and engineering experience, but thin on measured performance. The works below span that range.

\paragraph{Feature and architecture surveys.} The most comprehensive cross-implementation study to date is \emph{A Survey of Dataspace Connector Implementations}, which compares implementations on supported features, protocols, and architecture~\citep{dam2024surveydataspaceconnectorimplementations}. It is explicit that performance evaluation lies outside its scope and names it as future work~\citep{dam2024surveydataspaceconnectorimplementations}. Its architectural axes inform the connector profiles this thesis benchmarks (\cref{subsec:different-connectors,tab:connector-comparison}), but it provides no quantitative baseline.

\paragraph{Compliance reporting.} The most recent authoritative comparison, the IDSA Data Space Connector Report 2025, documents connectors that have been tested against the \gls{dsp} Technology Compatibility Kit (TCK)~\citep{idsaConnectorReport2025}. For six fully compliant connectors and one partially compliant one, it records the supported protocol version, the test outcome, and configuration and licensing metadata~\citep{idsaConnectorReport2025}. Its purpose is interoperability assurance, not performance: the report contains no throughput, latency, or resource measurements~\citep{idsaConnectorReport2025}. Even the field's official comparison therefore stops at \emph{whether} a connector conforms, not \emph{how fast} it does so.

\paragraph{Engineering experience.} \emph{Evolving Data Space Technologies: Lessons Learned from an IDS Connector Reference Implementation} covers identity and data management, secure communication, contract administration, and prototypical usage-control enforcement~\citep{pampus2022evolving}. The account is qualitative~--~an engineering retrospective rather than a measured evaluation~--~and concerns the earlier, \gls{ids}-native connector that predates the \gls{dsp} and is distinct from the Fraunhofer ISST EDC examined here (\cref{subsec:different-connectors}). It documents \emph{why} connector design is hard, not how design choices translate into performance. Recent architectural work continues this pattern: \emph{A Service Architecture for Dataspaces} proposes a service abstraction layer over the \gls{edc}, advancing connector capability without evaluating its performance~\citep{arnold2025servicearchitecture}.

\paragraph{Performance evaluation.} The one genuinely performance-oriented study, \emph{Evaluating the Performance Impact of Data Sovereignty Features on Data Spaces}, compares cloud-native data-sharing services from three hyperscalers against dataspace services delivered through two connector implementations, the Dataspace Connector and the Prometheus-X Dataspace Connector~\citep{evaluatingtheperformanceimpactofdatasovereignty}. The study reports substantial differences both between cloud providers (up to 187\%) and between the two connector implementations (56\% on average)~\citep{evaluatingtheperformanceimpactofdatasovereignty}, establishing that connector choice has a first-order effect on performance. Two characteristics distinguish it from this thesis: the systems compared are not \gls{edc}-derived, and the study isolates the single dimension of data-sovereignty overhead against a cloud-native baseline rather than profiling latency, throughput, scalability, and resource use across a set of \gls{dsp}-conformant connectors.


\section{Gap Analysis}\label{sec:gap-analysis}

Two gaps emerge from the preceding sections, and they compound.

The first is a \emph{coverage} gap. Mature comparative benchmarks exist for data-serving, messaging, and microservice systems~\citep{cooper2010ycsb, dizdarevic2023mqttbrokers, gan2019deathstarbench}, but none targets dataspace connectors. The connector literature that does exist is feature- and compliance-centric~\citep{dam2024surveydataspaceconnectorimplementations, idsaConnectorReport2025} or qualitative~\citep{pampus2022evolving, arnold2025servicearchitecture}, and the sole performance study compares non-\gls{edc} connectors along a single dimension~\citep{evaluatingtheperformanceimpactofdatasovereignty}. To the best of our knowledge, no published study reports a cross-implementation performance benchmark of \gls{edc}-derived, \gls{dsp}-conformant connectors. \Cref{tab:related-work-comparison} summarises this landscape.

The second is a \emph{methodological} gap. The benchmarks above measure either synchronous request--response exchanges or single-message publish/subscribe; none accommodates the asynchronous, stateful \gls{dsp} negotiation-and-transfer cycle (\cref{sec:dsp}), whose end-to-end latency spans two state machines across the provider-side and consumer-side runtimes. The reproducibility principles of \emph{Methodological Principles for Reproducible Performance Evaluation in Cloud Computing}~\citep{papadopoulos2021reproducible} and the empirical standard for benchmarking (\cref{sec:benchmarking-fundamentals}) supply the methodological scaffolding, but neither has yet been instantiated for the dataspace domain.

This thesis addresses both gaps. It defines a reproducible, \gls{gqm}-structured benchmark that exercises four \gls{dsp}-conformant connectors (\cref{subsec:different-connectors,tab:connector-comparison}) under common workloads, measuring end-to-end latency, throughput, scalability under concurrency, and resource consumption~--~thereby answering \textbf{TRQ1}~--~and interprets the measured differences against the connectors' architectural choices to answer \textbf{TRQ2} (\cref{sec:research-questions}). The benchmark design follows in \Cref{chap:methodology}, the deployed configuration in \Cref{chap:implementation}, and the results in \Cref{chap:results-evaluation} returns to the studies surveyed here to position this thesis's findings against them.
