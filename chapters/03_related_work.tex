\chapter{Related Work}\label{chap:related-work}

\Cref{chap:background} establishes what performance means for a dataspace connector and how a sound benchmark is constructed. This chapter surveys the studies that have applied those ideas, both in adjacent domains and to dataspace connectors specifically, and identifies the gap this thesis addresses. \Cref{sec:rw-data-exchange} reviews comparative benchmarks of competing data-exchange and data-serving systems, and \cref{sec:rw-connector-studies} examines the connector-specific literature. \Cref{sec:gap-analysis} consolidates both into the gap that motivates \textbf{TRQ1} and \textbf{TRQ2} (\cref{sec:research-questions}).


\section{Benchmarks for Data Exchange Systems}\label{sec:rw-data-exchange}

Comparing architecturally different systems that implement a common interface is a recurring problem in systems research, and benchmarking is its established empirical answer~\citep{hasselbring2021benchmarking}. The comparative benchmarks of adjacent domains illustrate the benchmarking fundamentals of \Cref{sec:benchmarking-fundamentals} most clearly. This thesis inherits its design template from them, even though none of their workloads transfers directly to a federated data-exchange protocol.

\textbf{Data-serving systems.} The Yahoo! Cloud Serving Benchmark (YCSB) is the canonical instrument for comparing key--value and cloud-serving stores~\citep{cooper2010ycsb}. Its design contributes two ideas this thesis reuses. First, it separates an extensible workload generator from a fixed set of core workloads (read-heavy, update-heavy, read-modify-write, and range-scan mixes) so that systems with very different internals are exercised through one identical interface~\citep{cooper2010ycsb}. Second, it reports performance not as a single figure but as latency-versus-throughput curves and as scaling behaviour under growing load~\citep{cooper2010ycsb}, the distribution-aware reporting that \Cref{subsec:performance-metrics} adopts. YCSB targets storage systems rather than a multi-party exchange protocol, so its workloads do not apply to a dataspace connector. What transfers is the abstraction: one common workload that exercises heterogeneous systems identically.

\textbf{Messaging middleware.} The closest structural analogue to this thesis is \emph{Engineering and Experimentally Benchmarking Open Source MQTT Broker Implementations}~\citep{dizdarevic2023mqttbrokers}. It compares five competing implementations of one standardised messaging protocol: Mosquitto, EMQX, RabbitMQ, VerneMQ, and HiveMQ. The study measures response-time overhead across payload sizes and degraded network conditions (latency, packet loss, jitter) on both AMD64 and ARM64 hosts, using an automated, containerised, and fully reproducible deployment~\citep{dizdarevic2023mqttbrokers}. Two findings carry over. First, the performance of implementations of one protocol varies materially with implementation choice, payload, and environment~\citep{dizdarevic2023mqttbrokers}. That is the premise which makes a cross-implementation connector comparison worthwhile. Second, an automated, containerised harness is the practical vehicle for a fair comparison. The difference from the present work lies in the unit of work. MQTT is a near-synchronous publish/subscribe exchange, whereas a \gls{dsp} interaction is the asynchronous, multi-message negotiation-and-transfer cycle of \cref{sec:dsp}. That cycle complicates both workload definition and latency attribution.

\textbf{Microservice systems and reproducibility.} A dataspace connector is not a monolith but a distributed system of cooperating services: control plane, data plane, identity, and catalog (\cref{subsec:edc}). DeathStarBench is a benchmark suite of representative microservice applications. It shows that inter-service network processing grows to a large share of execution time in such systems, and that this shifts the resource bottleneck away from where a single-tier analysis would place it~\citep{gan2019deathstarbench}. This is a direct caution for connector measurement: end-to-end latency must be attributed across cooperating services rather than to any one runtime, as \Cref{subsec:performance-metrics} requires. Comparability across studies is itself a methodological target. \emph{Methodological Principles for Reproducible Performance Evaluation in Cloud Computing} sets out eight principles for it, combining best practice from neighbouring fields with cloud-specific concerns and an explicit time--accuracy trade-off~\citep{papadopoulos2021reproducible}. This thesis treats reproducibility as a first-class requirement on that basis (\cref{sec:reproducibility}).

These benchmarks demonstrate that comparative, implementation-level performance evaluation is a mature method in storage, messaging, and microservice systems. No comparable instrument yet exists for dataspace connectors, which \Cref{sec:rw-connector-studies} examines next.


\section{Performance Studies on Dataspace Connectors}\label{sec:rw-connector-studies}

The connector-specific literature is substantial on features, compliance, and engineering experience, but thin on measured performance. The works below span that range.

\textbf{Feature and architecture surveys.} The most comprehensive cross-implementation study to date is \emph{A Survey of Dataspace Connector Implementations}, which compares implementations on supported features, protocols, and architecture~\citep{dam2024surveydataspaceconnectorimplementations}. It is explicit that performance evaluation lies outside its scope and names it as future work~\citep{dam2024surveydataspaceconnectorimplementations}. Its architectural axes inform the connector profiles this thesis benchmarks (\cref{subsec:different-connectors,tab:connector-comparison}), but it provides no quantitative baseline.

\textbf{Compliance reporting.} The most recent authoritative comparison, the IDSA Data Space Connector Report 2025, documents connectors that have been tested against the \gls{dsp} Technology Compatibility Kit (TCK)~\citep{idsaConnectorReport2025}. For six fully compliant connectors and one partially compliant one, it records the supported protocol version, the test outcome, and configuration and licensing metadata~\citep{idsaConnectorReport2025}. Its purpose is interoperability assurance, not performance: the report contains no throughput, latency, or resource measurements~\citep{idsaConnectorReport2025}. Even the field's official comparison therefore stops at whether a connector conforms, not how fast it does so.

\textbf{Engineering experience.} \emph{Evolving Data Space Technologies: Lessons Learned from an IDS Connector Reference Implementation} covers identity and data management, secure communication, contract administration, and prototypical usage-control enforcement~\citep{pampus2022evolving}. The account is qualitative, an engineering retrospective rather than a measured evaluation. It also concerns the earlier \gls{ids}-native connector, which predates the \gls{dsp} and is a different system from the Fraunhofer ISST EDC examined here (\cref{subsec:different-connectors}). It documents why connector design is hard, not how design choices translate into performance. Recent architectural work continues this pattern: \emph{A Service Architecture for Dataspaces} proposes a service abstraction layer over the \gls{edc}, advancing connector capability without evaluating its performance~\citep{arnold2025servicearchitecture}.

\textbf{Performance evaluation.} The one genuinely performance-oriented study is \emph{Evaluating the Performance Impact of Data Sovereignty Features on Data Spaces}~\citep{evaluatingtheperformanceimpactofdatasovereignty}. It compares cloud-native data-sharing services from three hyperscalers against dataspace services delivered through two connector implementations, the Dataspace Connector and the Prometheus-X Dataspace Connector. The study reports substantial differences both between cloud providers (up to 187\%) and between the two connector implementations (56\% on average)~\citep{evaluatingtheperformanceimpactofdatasovereignty}, establishing that connector choice has a first-order effect on performance. Two characteristics distinguish it from this thesis. The systems compared are not \gls{edc}-derived. And the study isolates the single dimension of data-sovereignty overhead against a cloud-native baseline, instead of profiling latency, throughput, scalability, and resource use across a set of \gls{dsp}-conformant connectors.


\section{Gap Analysis}\label{sec:gap-analysis}

Two gaps emerge from the preceding sections, and they compound.

The first is a \emph{coverage} gap. Mature comparative benchmarks exist for data-serving, messaging, and microservice systems~\citep{cooper2010ycsb, dizdarevic2023mqttbrokers, gan2019deathstarbench}, but none targets dataspace connectors. The connector literature that does exist is feature- and compliance-centric~\citep{dam2024surveydataspaceconnectorimplementations, idsaConnectorReport2025} or qualitative~\citep{pampus2022evolving, arnold2025servicearchitecture}, and the sole performance study compares non-\gls{edc} connectors along a single dimension~\citep{evaluatingtheperformanceimpactofdatasovereignty}. To the best of our knowledge, no published study reports a cross-implementation performance benchmark of \gls{edc}-derived, \gls{dsp}-conformant connectors. \Cref{tab:related-work-comparison} summarises these studies.

The second is a \emph{methodological} gap. The benchmarks above measure either synchronous request--response exchanges or single-message publish/subscribe. None accommodates the asynchronous, stateful \gls{dsp} negotiation-and-transfer cycle (\cref{sec:dsp}), whose end-to-end latency spans two state machines across the provider-side and consumer-side runtimes. The reproducibility principles of \emph{Methodological Principles for Reproducible Performance Evaluation in Cloud Computing}~\citep{papadopoulos2021reproducible} and the empirical standard for benchmarking (\cref{sec:benchmarking-fundamentals}) supply the methodological scaffolding, but neither has yet been instantiated for the dataspace domain.

This thesis addresses both gaps. It defines a reproducible, \gls{gqm}-structured benchmark that exercises three \gls{dsp}-conformant connectors (\cref{subsec:different-connectors,tab:connector-comparison}) under common workloads. Measuring end-to-end latency, throughput, scalability under concurrency, and resource consumption answers \textbf{TRQ1}. Interpreting the measured differences against the connectors' architectural choices answers \textbf{TRQ2} (\cref{sec:research-questions}). The benchmark design follows in \cref{chap:methodology} and the deployed configuration in \cref{chap:implementation}. \Cref{chap:results-evaluation} reports the results and returns to the studies surveyed here, positioning this thesis's findings against them.
